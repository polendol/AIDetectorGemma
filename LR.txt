2.1. First Exposure (FE) to a New Language
     Previous Second Language Acquisition (SLA) research focused mainly on en route or al fine learners, who are already familiar with the Target Language (TL), leading to a scarcity of information about acquisition processes in ab initio learners, i. e., those who are exposed to a new language for the very first time. On the contrary, studies concerned with the very initial stages of acquisition, also called First Exposure (FE) studies, deal with acquisition “from the very first moment of contact with the TL and within the first seconds, minutes and hours of subsequent exposure” (Rast, 2008, p. 29) and are concerned with a) the previous linguistic knowledge the novice learners bring to the table at initial stages of language processing, b) the impact of learners’ individual differences at initial stages of learning and c) the influence of input on initial processing. Thus, FE studies seek to answer two general questions: how do the resources that learners of a new language bring to the task affect learning, and what are the mechanisms that govern language learning? (Indefrey & Gullberg, 2010). To answer these questions, FE studies so far focused on different aspects that potentially affect processing, such as TL, input type, etc. However, they all share a set of common characteristics, which are: a concern with implicit learning processes and mechanisms; a focus on how those processes are related to the input; a total control of the TL input and a need to ensure that the participants have no possibility of using their previous language knowledge or long-term memory when tested (Carroll, 2013). 
Investigation of ab initio learners is crucial as researchers agree on the fact that studying these learners has a potential to explore the rules reigning the mechanism of breaking into a new language (Rast, 2008; Indefrey & Gullberg, 2010) and give us a better insight into second language processing and learning. For instance, Han and Rast (2014) argue that not studying language processing in learners at the very initial stages of acquisition can lead to an incomplete picture when theorising about language processing overall. However, in his epilogue to a volume by Han and Rast (2014) dedicated to FE studies, VanPatten suggests “compared to ultimate attainment or end-state research, the topic of what novice learners do during the earliest stages of acquisition is an area that has not received the interest from researchers it deserves” (p. 193). In fact, even though adult second language (L2) learning is slower and more difficult than first language (L1) acquisition by children, McLaughlin et al. (2004) showed that minimal L2 instruction can produce a significant change in adult ab initio learners. Thus, the following questions arise: how can information on language learning at FE contribute to a better understanding of SLA and how effective are adult novice learners at language processing? 
Research into the initial stages of Second Language Acquisition (SLA) gained significant momentum in the 1990s, catalysed by emerging generative theories regarding the "starting point of non-native grammatical knowledge" (Schwartz & Sprouse, 1996, p. 1). During this period, the debate centred on two primary orientations: Universal Grammar (UG) and the First Language (L1). The UG implies an innate linguistic knowledge that humans possess independently of their L1 and that is activated when learning an L2 (Hawkins, 2007). Conversely, the Full Transfer Hypothesis (Schwartz & Sprouse, 1996) posits that the starting point of L2 acquisition is the final state of the L1 acquisition. In this view, novice learners always rely on their L1 grammar when formulating their interlanguage and only use UG when the L1-based processing fails. Several alternative frameworks further refine this L1-to-L2 relationship. For instance, the Valueless Feature Hypothesis (Eubank, 1993) posited that the L1 grammar is almost entirely transferred to the interlanguage. In contrast, the Minimal Trees Hypothesis (Vainikka & Young-Scholten, 1996) argues that transfer is restricted to lexical categories, with functional projections developing only as the learner acquires new lexical items. However, a later meticulous analysis of the studies accounting for the transfer theories revealed that very often participants in the experiments had already been exposed to the TL before, and so the research did not really examine the starting point of language processing (Rast, 2010). 
Later on, Pienemann (1998) put forward his Processability Theory (or Developmentally Moderated Transfer Hypothesis) stating that L1 transfer in the initial stages of L2 acquisition is limited to the processability of the target structure in the L1. In this way, his work casted doubts on the previous generativist research that often focused on similar structures (e.g., English, and German negation, Eubank, 1993) and, more importantly, stressed the crucial role of the ‘initial state’ in language processing in any SLA theory. Subsequently, Klein & Perdue (1997) identified the ‘prebasic’ and the ‘basic’ varieties of early L2 learners’ interlanguage features that did not seem to be accounted by the L1 grammar nor by the L2 grammar learned so far. Finally, Carroll’s (2001) Autonomous Induction Theory (AIT) argued that L1 perceptual and processing procedures are transferred as the starting point of L2 analysis at FE. Such a theoretical fragmentation revealed the need for a more systematic investigation into how second languages are processed upon FE to account for this critical window when the learner first encounters the TL and perceives it as little more than undifferentiated "noise."
Despite the fact that FE studies are a quite recent field of research in SLA, they consistently manage to show that adult language learners are highly effective at breaking into a new language and that “the adult brain is clearly capable of plasticity and of rapidly adjusting to new learning experiences” (Gullberg et al., 2012, p. 258). As previously mentioned, studies vary widely not only in their L1-TL pairings and the specific linguistic ability tested (e.g., vocabulary, syntax, or general comprehension), but also in how they deal with additional factors, with relatively few studies accounting for individual learner differences. This broad range of designs complicates cross-study comparison and the exploration of the nuances of how learners navigate input at initial stage. Consequently, to effectively synthesize these findings, a clear analytical framework is required. Given that the mode of delivery fundamentally shapes the early linguistic experience, the input modality serves as the most effective criterion for categorization. By grouping these studies according to their specific input types, we isolate can more effectively how distinct modalities influence the initial stages of language acquisition.
2.1.1. Research Paradigms [PO1.1]in FE
Several definitions of input are complementary in SLA. Chaudron (1985) defines input as “raw data from which learners derive both meaning and awareness of the rules and the structures of the target language” (p. 3). As defined by Sharwood-Smith (1993), input is “any processable language data which are made available by chance or by design, to the language learner” (p. 167). More recently, Barcroft (2015) describes input as simply any sample of target language available to learners. 
Theoretical frameworks in SLA assign distinct roles to input depending on the theoretical framework underpinning the research. For example, according to the Full Transfer Hypothesis, the initial state of the L2 was considered to be the ‘final’ state of the L1, which implies that L2 input would be considered as the linguistic material used to reset parameters (Schwartz & Sprouse, 1996). However, with the emergence of interactionist hypotheses, input was reconsidered as an essential element of SLA (Gass, 1997). Input was additionally enhanced by the emergentist views, as well as usage-based theories that considered input as key when obtaining statistical information (e.g. on word frequency) through which learning was taking place (Ellis, 1998; MacWhinney, 1999). Nowadays, researchers underline that the crucial role of input in SLA is often neglected: Carroll (2000, p. 1) stated that input was “one of the most under-researched and under-theorised aspects of L2 acquisition”, while Flege’s (2009) Give input a chance! argues that more research is still needed to determine the actual role of input in L2 learning and that better L2 input measures (or estimates) could provide us with a clearer insight into the matter.

Given the prominent role of the input for SLA, a question arises on how vocabulary is processed from the input at first encounter. Some researchers suggest that form is processed before the meaning (i.e. Klein, 1986), so that the meaning is attached to the form. Klein's ‘problem of analysis’ tenets (1986) (e. g. Zwitserlood, 2000; Rast, 2008; Gullberg et al.,2010) are concerned with three major elements: 
-	The segmentation of the continuous speech stream to identify words.
-	The identification of relevant meaning in the environment that can be mapped onto the sound strings.
-	The generalisation beyond the input stimuli.                                                                                                                                                                
Thus, FE studies following this approach are concerned with the learners’ ability to segment words from the sound stream, map them to their meaning and, eventually, generalise them to formulate a ‘rule’ (Gullberg et al.,2010). 
Conversely, another stage of FE studies aligns with VanPatten’s input processing principles (1996, 2004, 2007) which posit that L2 learners tend to follow a set of principles when exposed to a L2 input:
1)	Principle 1. The Primacy of Meaning Principle: Learners process input for meaning before they process it for form.
1.a) The Primacy of Content Words Principle: Learners process content words in the input before anything else.
1.b) The Lexical Preference Principle: Learners will tend to rely on lexical items as opposed to grammatical form to get meaning when both encode the same semantic information.
1.c) The Preference for Non-redundancy Principle: Learners are more likely to process non-redundant meaningful forms before they process redundant meaningful forms.
1.d) The Meaning-before-non-meaning Principle: Learners are more likely to process meaningful grammatical forms before non-meaningful forms irrespective of redundancy.
1.e) The Availability of Resources Principle: For learners to process either redundant meaningful grammatical forms or non-meaningful forms, the processing of overall sentential meaning must not drain available processing resources.
1.f) The Sentence Location Principle: Learners tend to process items in sentence initial position before those in final position and those in medial position.
2)	Principle 2. The First Noun Principle: Learners tend to process the first noun or pronoun they encounter in a sentence as subject or agent.
2.a) The Lexical Semantics Principle: Learners may rely on lexical semantics, where possible, instead of word order to interpret sentences.
2.b) The Event Probability Principle: Learners may rely on event probabilities, where possible, instead of word order to interpret sentences.
2.c) The Context Constraint Principle: Learners may rely less on the First Noun Principle if preceding context constrains the possible interpretation of a clause or sentence.
Among these principles, the first one (the primacy of meaning principle) had a major influence on research on ab initio learners. For instance, Han and Peverly (2007) used a beginning level textbook in Norwegian (TL) to test a group of participants with no knowledge of the TL on their comprehension (through meaning recall) and recognition of form (through gap-filling). As opposed to the primacy of meaning principle, the participants scored consistently better at gap-filling than at written-recall, showing that their focus in the task was mainly on form. The authors justified the findings claiming that the input was linguistically and functionally insufficient to provide the learners with an aid to their comprehension, which forced them to apply a bottom-up strategy in order to ‘crack the code’ (Han & Peverly, 2007, p. 30). Similar results were demonstrated in a Han and Sun’s (2014) partial replication of Han and Peverly (2007), in which other than focusing on meaning and form focus on Norwegian text processing, researchers also examined potential impact of simultaneous and sequential processing. Thus, while one group of participants completed both gap-filling and written recall exercises following reading, second group followed a sequential design, in which they completed two readings followed by one exercise each. Once again, results revealed that novice learners tended to apply a form-based approach rather focusing on meaning. The same conclusion was reached by Park & Han (2008): both Japanese and English native speakers exposed to a text in Korean (typologically similar to Japanese, but dissimilar to English) showed focus on frequent or salient items (i.e., formal aspects). However, it is important to underline that in a subsequent task the tendencies were divergent (Japanese speakers showed more interest in the content, as opposed to English speakers’ interest in the form). 
	Beyond the cognitive mechanisms of word learning, the specific nature of the linguistic input serves as a determinant of acquisition. The following sections synthesizes FE research by input modality to highlight how varying linguistic environments influence acquisition at the very initial stages.
2.1.1.1. Studies with artificial input 
As total control over authentic input may be impossible (Flege, 2009), using an artificial language in FE studies is “inherently attractive” (Indefrey & Gullberg, 2010, p. 1). Therefore, artificial languages have been used as a solution to the problem of controlling and measuring input. 
It is worth noticing that the debate concerning the use of artificial languages at FE extends to other fields of SLA, such as the question whether artificial languages mirror natural languages and can be used to make inferences on real language learning. On the one hand, “a growing body of empirical evidence suggests that the mechanisms involved in artificial language learning and in structured sequence processing are shared with those of natural language acquisition and natural language processing” (Folia et al., 2010, p. 189). On the other hand, it is hard to claim that an artificial language can reflect the complexity of fully-fledged natural languages. 
Given its benefits for input control, the first psycholinguistic studies examining initial processing of an L2 employed mainly artificial languages. Although these studies are not considered FE studies per se, they are relevant for further FE research for their statistical exploration of input processing at the initial stages of learning (i.e., Reber, 1967). The first three proper FE studies using artificial languages were published in 1997. The first one by DeKeyser (1997) used ‘Autopractan’ (an invented language) to explore the effect of practice on the acquisition of L2 morphology. The two groups in the study received 22 hours of exposure in which they were given the grammar and vocabulary rules of this artificial language, as well as computerised practice in these rules. However, the practice differed in its focus on comprehension or on production. Results revealed that practice in SLA has a strictly skill specific effect, meaning that “performance in comprehension and production is severely reduced if only the opposite skill was practised” (DeKeyser, 1997, p. 213). 
The second study from 1997 by De Graaff used an artificial eXperanto language to investigate the impact of explicit vs. implicit grammatical instruction on complexity of L2 structures in acquisition. After 15 hours of exposure, Dutch native speakers completed a grammaticality judgement task, a gap-filling task and a vocabulary translation task. Results revealed that explicit instruction enhanced acquisition. Finally, Yang and Givón (1997) compared the processing of an artificial language (‘Keki’) from a simplified (pidgin) input and from a fully grammatical input. Two groups of participants received 50 hours of exposure of simplified input or fully grammatical input, after which they were tested in a variety of tasks, including word recognition, word semantic priming measure and word translation for vocabulary acquisition, as well as sentence elicitation, grammaticality judgments, picture description and narrative translation and comprehension for grammar acquisition. Findings showed that “learners who received simplified pidgin input lagged behind learners who received fully grammatical input in both grammar acquisition and related comprehension skills” (p. 188).  What is more, they concluded that SLA research in laboratory conditions is recommendable and reliable, yet they acknowledge that they do not consider their results generalizable to real language learning given “Keki was a simplified, miniature, artificial language” (p. 191).
Consequently, although artificial languages have been used in FE studies in the past, some major issues concerning their ecological validity arose. For instance, Carroll (2013) points out that “we should ask if the use of artificial languages misinterprets the task the L2 learner faces” (p. 139), and Indefrey & Gullberg (2010) suggest that “learning properties of an artificial language might just miss essential aspects of natural second-language learning” (p. 2). On the other hand, Rast (2008) underlines the need to validate FE studies with artificial languages, as “the results of both [artificial and natural language input studies] taken together should allow us to make comparisons and come to conclusion about what learners do with their linguistic input, be it natural or artificial” (p. 42).
In sum, although previous studies in artificial languages are undoubtedly a relevant contribution to initial L2 processing research, research with natural language is crucial to fully explore the cognitive mechanisms of breaking into the new language. This can be found in L2 classes (regular instruction) or in naturalistic settings. 
2.1.1.2. Classroom input
Receiving L2 input in an L2 language class is undoubtedly one of the most common practices for many language learners. Although the nature of learning may vary (e.g., implicit vs. explicit learning), this type of setting gives the researchers some control over the input, as the lessons can be previously scripted and instructors can adapt their discourse according to the study’s needs. For this reason, several FE studies with natural language input opted for classroom settings to investigate various aspects of initial L2 acquisition. 
One of the first FE studies in a classroom setting was conducted by Lalleman (1999), who exposed novice learners (various L1s) to Dutch for the period of 3-6 weeks. The aim of the experiment was to test learners’ sensitivity to gapping, a grammatical phenomenon in Dutch that is not usually explicitly taught in L2 classroom settings despite being very frequent in speech. The results revealed that, independently of the participants’ linguistic background, they performed better than expected in the grammaticality judgement test. In this way, Lalleman concluded that Schwartz and Sprouse’s (1996) Full Transfer (or Full Access) theory failed to explain initial processing in this case, given the fact that learners in the study did not seem to use their L1 knowledge to deal with the gapping phenomenon. 
Similarly, Hendriks and Prodeau (1999, 2000) investigated acquisition of another grammatical phenomenon present in Dutch: finite verb placement. This time, all the participants shared the same L1 (French), but some of them had prior knowledge of German as L2. Results revealed that the knowledge of German assisted the acquisition of the grammatical feature of Dutch, as only the learners that were learning Dutch as L3 were able to discover the target verb placement rule. 
As for 21st century research,  McLaughlin et al. (2004) analysed brain activity to determine the rate of L2 learning at early stages. 18 university learners were exposed to different numbers of instruction of French as TL. Their ability of discriminating between words and pseudowords was tested after 14, 68, and 138 hours of classroom exposure.  A control group of 8 non-learners was included. Their brain activity during task was measured through event-related potentials (ERPs). Results showed that learning took place after as little as 14 hours of exposure, and that form seems to be processed before meaning. Thus, researchers conclude the following:
The earliest evidence of learning, even before changes in overt lexicality judgments, is seen in ERPs to L2 words and pseudowords. This early learning might involve elemental aspects of linguistic knowledge (e.g., knowledge about word forms) that serve as prerequisites to linguistic competence. Our results indicate that adult L2 learning is not uniformly slow and laborious; some aspects of the language are acquired with remarkable speed. (McLaughlin et al., 2004, p. 2)
Another relevant contribution to FE research in classroom settings is found in the VILLA (Varieties of Initial Learners in Language Acquisition) project (Dimroth et al., 2013) involving a number of studies with novice adults and child learners from different linguistic backgrounds (Dutch, English, French, German and Italian). The focus was on the first hours of exposure to Polish input in communicative classroom settings (with various testing times throughout the exposure period). This longitudinal study aimed at exploring acquisition and processing of different linguistic aspects (phonology, morpho-syntax, discourse-pragmatics), taking into account participants' individual differences (i.e., age, L1, etc.) and the amount of metalinguistic information provided during the course. Additionally, the role of input in acquisition was examined, and children and adults’ processing mechanisms were compared. Input was controlled by employing a Polish instructor with ample linguistic knowledge but with no teaching experience to ensure continuity in the methods. The observation period took place over 10 days, and the last day learners did not receive any new input: they were engaged in a conversation on the topics they had learnt about during the course to activate their language skills. All the sessions were recorded and transcribed. It is important to emphasize that, for the first time in FE studies, the VILLA project explored a wide range of individual differences as a possible factor affecting initial processing. Other than age and L1, the participants were tested on their nonverbal, personality, memory span, working memory, executive function, cognitive style, learning style, motivation, meta-linguistic awareness, phonological memory/awareness and L2 vocabulary size. The most influential studies within this project are reviewed below.
Rast and Dommergues (2003) exposed French native speakers (n = 16) with no previous knowledge of any Slavic language to 4 and subsequently 8 hours of instruction of Polish to investigate initial L2 learning. Lexical factors controlled in their analysis were word length (measured in number of syllables), word stress, phonemic distance from the L1 and word transparency. Additionally, two contextually determined factors were analysed: the position of the word in the sentence and its frequency in input. The experiment included 113 target words, instruction time of six weeks and the instructor that applied a communicative approach (avoiding metalanguage). The participants performed a repetition task (sentence repetition containing the 113 target words) prior to instruction (one group, n = 8), 4 hours of instruction (new group, n = 8) and 8 hours of instruction (the same group, n = 8). Results showed that the period of instruction had a significant effect on the performance of the repetition task. Input was also shown to have impact on almost every factor considered (word length, word stress, phonemic distance and transparency), except from words that were classified as ‘very transparent’ and the words in initial position in utterance. The effect of frequency on learning was observed only after 8 hours of instruction, and not earlier, suggesting that frequency may play a role only after enough exposure to the L2. In sum, the study suggests that the amount of input received, as well as additional factors, such as word length, word stress, phonemic distance to the L1, word transparency, position in the sentence and frequency, enhance leaning at initial stages of SLA. 
Rast (2008) investigated language processing at FE through a longitudinal study involving 127 participants: 96 FE informants, 19 learners attending a Polish course, and 12 native Polish speakers serving as a control group. Over a 15-week period, learners received a total of 8 hours of communicative instruction, with performance measured at three specific intervals: 1h30, 3h30, and 7 hours of exposure. The findings for the FE informants revealed an average 18% word recognition rate, which was notably dependent on the participants' L1 background. Analysis of the learner group’s speech perception showed that exposure time, phonemic distance, transparency, word position, and frequency significantly impacted results—though word length did not—while speech comprehension accuracy rose from 46% at 1h30 to 67% at 3h30, influenced by word transparency, sentence length, and SVO word order. Furthermore, grammatical processing tasks indicated a clear developmental trajectory: learners' preference for SVO structures increased from 81% to 95%, and participants gradually shifted their placement of negators and reflexive pronouns from L1 patterns toward target language norms. Ultimately, the author concludes that these results disprove the argument that L1 transfer alone cannot explain how grammatical features are processed and that learners begin to parse complex structures of a novel language well before they are able to generate them.
Shoemaker and Rast (2013) extended previous research by Rast and Dommergues (2003) to investigate factors influencing initial word processing within a 6.5-hour intensive Polish classroom setting. The study involved 18 French native speakers with no prior exposure to Slavic languages, who were tested on their ability to identify words within sentences both before and after instruction. Analysis focused on three specific input-related factors: word transparency, frequency, and position within the utterance. Results confirmed that word transparency and prosodic position are critical determinants of early recognition; highly transparent words were successfully identified even at the initial testing phase, indicating that novice learners actively leverage their L1, while items located at the edges of prosodic domains proved most salient. Conversely, frequency did not significantly influence recognition after 6.5 hours; the researchers attributed this to a ceiling effect, noting that high-frequency words were already well-recognized at the first testing time, while the 6.5-hour duration provided insufficient exposure for learners to process low-frequency forms. Ultimately, the study confirms that adult learners are capable of extracting lexical information after very brief exposure and validates the classroom as an effective environment for controlling and measuring the relationship between input and SLA.
In summary, studies conducted within the framework of the VILLA project were pioneering in demonstrating not only the capacity of novice learners to extract linguistic information from minimal classroom input but also in isolating the specific factors that modulate this acquisition process. Collectively, these studies highlight that the initial stages of SLA are governed by a complex interplay of variables, and that the adult brain is highly plastic, capable of identifying linguistic patterns and abstracting complex structures long before reaching proficiency. 
Beyond the VILLA project, another FE study that used a classroom setting to investigate how the input is processed in the initial stages of acquisition was conducted by Ellis and Sagarra (2010). The experiment was meant to explore the effects of long-term learned attention on acquisition of morphological cues in learners with morphologically poor L1s. Four groups were compared in the study: a control group of native English speakers with no knowledge of Spanish, another control group of L1 Spanish speakers, and two groups of native English speakers learning Spanish at beginner and intermediate level. The experiment consisted in reading sentences in Spanish with lexical (adverbs) and morphological (verbal inflection) cues to temporal references and estimating the regression times (RTs) spent on verbs or adverbs with use of eye-tracker. The control group of English native speakers read the same sentences in English. The comparison of RTs showed that, as expected, Spanish native speakers spent more time looking at verbal cues than English native speakers with no knowledge of Spanish who gazed more at adverbial cues. As far as the learners of L2 Spanish are concerned, although both groups spent more time looking at adverbial cues (as usual in their L1), intermediate learners showed longer RTs at morphological cues than beginner learners. These results suggest that the effect of long-term learned attention was present in both learner groups, however, was slightly lower in intermediate learners than in beginners. Overall, researchers conclude that their findings reflect the phenomena of blocking of later experienced cues by earlier experienced ones in adults learners, given that “as a result of their L1 experience, they know that there are reliable and salient lexical means for expressing time (e.g., yesterday) that are far simpler than the nonsalient and ambiguous morphological means that vary in complex ways by person and number” (Ellis and Sagarra, 2010, p. 102). 
Finally, Carroll and Widjaja (2013) used classroom settings to analyse acquisition of exponents of number at FE. Adult English native speakers (n = 29) were exposed to Indonesian, a language in which there are three possible ways of expressing numbers (bare noun phrases, reduplication, and numeral with classifier construction). The experiment comprised two sessions: the first involved training and assessment across three structures (bare noun phrases, reduplication, and numeral classifiers), followed by a NoPrompt Elicitation task. Two weeks later, a second session featured a re-test and a production task using novel items. Results showed that participants were able to acquire all three number exponents relatively successfully in the TL. Thus, learners were not constrained by their L1 features in the analysis of input at FE. In addition, researchers conclude that participants were not “constrained to represent and produce only the sound–meaning mappings associated with lexical categories”, but “they can also rapidly acquire sound–meaning mappings associated with functional categories, (Carroll & Widjaja, 2013: p. 221). In sum, the study contributed to the research in language acquisition at FE by showing that ab initio learners are able not only to segment and map TL forms to their representations, but also to analyse TL morpho-syntax. 
While classroom-based FE research provides valuable insights into how learners process input in instructional, ecologically valid environments, these settings inherently imply significant 'noise,' which complicates the experimental control required to isolate the mechanisms of acquisition. One of the possible alternative proposed to address this challenge are laboratory-based studies. The following section reviews this body of research, detailing how laboratory-based input has been used to examine the mechanisms of acquisition at FE.
2.1.1.3. Input typology: laboratory studies.
FE studies conducted in laboratory settings are characterised by a strictly controlled input, teaching and/or learning process and testing for target structures. Participants usually undergo individual training block or exposure stage when they are exposed to a target linguistic element, which is immediately followed by testing. These settings are used to isolate the effect of specific variables, for instance, frequency, also called “input skewedness”, or inductive vs. deductive instruction. Some examples of laboratory FE studies are Ellis and Sagarra (2010) using Latin, Nakamura (2012) using Samoan, or McDonough and Trofimovich (2013, 2016, 2018) using Esperanto. 
Ellis and Sagarra (2010) investigated the impact of short-term and long-term learned attention on the acquisition of lexical (adverbial) versus morphological (verbal inflection) cues in adult learners. The research comprised a series of laboratory experiments (targeting Latin) involving native speakers of languages that rely primarily on either adverbial (English, Chinese) or morphological (Spanish, Russian) cues for temporal reference. Across the laboratory studies, the researchers found that L1 experience significantly dictates attentional focus; English and Chinese speakers consistently prioritized salient adverbial cues over complex morphological ones, reflecting a "blocking" effect. However, this attentional bias proved mutable: interventions such as targeted pretraining and typographical enhancement (highlighting cues in bold and blue text) successfully directed learners' attention toward morphological cues. Conversely, the control groups of L1 Spanish and Russian speakers consistently prioritized morphological cues, characteristic of their L1s. The findings conclude that while adult learners are initially affected by the learned attention patterns of their L1, instructions can effectively mitigate this bias and promote the processing of novel morphological features.
Nakamura (2012) investigated the acquisition of Samoan "appearance" (ergative) constructions in 137 ab initio learners representing five different L1 backgrounds (Japanese, Thai, Korean, Chinese, and American). The study manipulated three specific input variables: skewedness (frequency of specific tokens), consistency (embedding targets with concrete nouns or pronouns), and the order of frequent verbs. Following the memorization of 41 Samoan words, participants were assigned to one of eight experimental groups based on the specific combination of these variables and completed training blocks containing the target structures. Assessment included a forced-choice comprehension task, a guided production task, and a questionnaire on awareness and perceived difficulty. Results showed that all groups achieved a success rate exceeding 50% across all linguistic tasks. However, the input variables showed varying influences: while skewedness did not affect comprehension, it had a negative impact on guided production; consistency negatively affected both comprehension tasks; and verb order influenced the comprehension of the appearance construction.
Finally, McDonough and Trofimovich (2013, 2016, 2018) conducted a series of laboratory studies to isolate the cognitive and instructional factors governing initial states of SLA In their 2013 study, they examined the influence of input distribution (skewed vs. balanced) and instructional type (inductive vs. deductive) on the acquisition of Esperanto word order (SVO, OVS) among 98 Thai speakers. Findings indicated that the combination of balanced input and deductive instruction yielded the most significant learning benefits. Building on this, McDonough and Trofimovich (2016) explored the role of statistical learning and working memory in three experiments involving 140 Thai EFL students. Testing both novel (Esperanto) and known (English) linguistic patterns, they found that while statistical learning consistently facilitated the processing of novel morphological structures, working memory had no significant impact on acquisition. Although participants performed accurately when relying on meaning, performance was higher on SVO structures, and following replications highlighted variability in the predictive power of statistical learning. Finally, McDonough and Trofimovich (2018) revisited these findings to evaluate the role of salience, concluding that input-based salience (e.g., skewedness) is less critical for morphology learning at FE than learner-related salience, such as previous linguistic knowledge. Collectively, this body of work suggests that FE acquisition is not merely dependent on input frequency or simple "exposure," but is mediated by complex interactions between cognitive mechanisms and the learner’s existing linguistic background.
Therefore, thanks to strictly controlled input, training and testing conditions, laboratory FE studies conducted so far provided crucial insights into adults’ early L2 acquisition. Results suggest that both input features, such as skewedness and or consistency, and learner features, such as linguistic experience and cognitive skills, play a fundamental role in L2 learning of adult novice learners. 
2.1.2. Summary of Early FE Findings
In sum, a considerable number of FE studies conducted during the last three decades consistently proved that adults are able to process a wide range of linguistic information from various types of input. The participants were able to successfully extract morphological (DeKeyser, 1997; Ellis & Sagarra, 2010; Dimroth et al., 2013), grammar (DeGraaff, 1997; Yang & Givón, 1997; Lalleman, 1999; Hendriks & Prodeau, 1999, 2000; Dimroth et al., 2013), and lexical information (Yang & Givón, 1997; Rast & Dommergues, 2003; Rast, 2008; Shoemaker & Rast, 2013) from classroom and laboratory input. However, to better reflect the ecological validity of real-world language encounters, research must account for kind of input: audiovisual (AV) input. With the advent of streaming platforms, video games and movies, learners gained an almost unlimited access to a rich and naturalistic FL input on daily basis. Consequently, learning a new language through exposure to AV input is becoming a trend among learners globally, a phenomenon which calls for the inclusion of AV input in the FE research.
2.2. Audiovisual Input in L2 Acquisition
2.2.1. Theoretical Foundations
Given [PO2.1]a growing accessibility of streaming channels and television promoting films, series, and games in foreign languages, it is of great interest for SLA research to examine its impact on L2 learning. AV input, which can be defined as simultaneous presentation of visual (video) and aural information, has been an object of research in SLA for around four decades, since when Price (1983) first showed its beneficial effect on L2 acquisition. Muñoz (2022) claims AV input optimal for L2 learning “because it can be comprehensible, authentic, limitless, engaging, adaptable and relevant” (p. 125), and Dressman (2020), among others, indicates that the combination of written, aural, and visual information increases input comprehensibility, as well as provides learners with studying material within and beyond the classroom. 
Through years of research in the field, several input-processing theories justifying the use of AV input in SLA research have been formulated. On one hand, Paivio’s (1986, 2007) Dual Coding Theory (DCT) and Mayer’s (2009, 2014) Cognitive Theory of Multimedia learning (CTML) coincide in pointing out that information received through different channels can be better learned and retained. According to DCT, human mind operates through two separate processing systems: the verbal system (Logogens) responsible for dealing with language-based information, and non-verbal or imagery system (Imagens) specialized in dealing with on-linguistic input elements, such as image, sound, or spatial information. The core hypothesis of the theory is that information stored in both the verbal and non-verbal systems is more likely to be retrieved than information stored in only one system. Because each system is independent, encoding information via "dual coding" creates two separate memory traces. This provides two different "paths" for retrieval: if the verbal memory trace fails, the imagery trace may still be accessible. Therefore, multimodal input combining both linguistic and non-verbal information presents an advantage of facilitating the deeper encoding required for meaningful learning. 
CTML premise is that instructional materials are most effective when designed in alignment with the architecture of the human mind. This theory is grounded in three core cognitive assumptions:
a. Dual-channel assumption: the human information processing system utilizes separate, independent channels for processing visual/pictorial and auditory/verbal information.
b. Limited-capacity assumption: each of these channels has a finite capacity to process information at any given time.
c. Active processing assumption: meaningful learning requires the learner to engage in a coordinated set of cognitive processes.
To facilitate learning, the theory puts forward five essential cognitive processes: selecting relevant words, selecting relevant graphics, organizing the selected words into a coherent verbal representation, organizing images into a coherent pictorial representation, and integrating these dual representations with one another and with prior knowledge. Additionally, the theory posits that effective multimedia design must balance cognitive demands by aligning instructional strategies with the learner's cognitive state. This can be achieved by, for instance, minimizing distractions to prevent overload of processing unrelated to the instructional objective, streamline the presentation of complex material to avoid overloading of the cognitive effort required to mentally represent the core material, or implement strategies that encourage the learner to engage deeply with the material to enhance cognitive effort aimed at making sense of the material and fostering understanding.
On the other hand, Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) (Chandler & Sweller, 1991; Sweller, 1994) argues that high cognitive load of AV input might affect processing negatively. Given the assumption of a limited capacity of working memory involved in input processing, the primary goal of CLT is to optimize instructional design, so that the cognitive load placed on working memory does not exceed its capacity, thereby facilitating the transfer of information into long-term memory. This can be achieved by reducing the “extraneous” cognitive load (unnecessary distractions), managing the “intrinsic” load (the inherent complexity of the material itself depending on the number of elements that must be processed simultaneously), and optimizing the “germane” load, or the mental effort dedicated to the processing, construction, and automation of schemas later committed to long-term memory. 
The efficacy of AV input is debated within the literature: while DCT and CTML suggest that channel diversity facilitates meaningful learning, CLT cautions that such input can impose an additional cognitive load that compromises WM capacity. This is especially relevant for beginner learners, for whom the high processing requirements of AV media may outweigh its potential pedagogical advantages. The studies discussed in the following section exemplify this debate, focusing specifically on how beginner L2 learners manage these cognitive demands during AV comprehension tasks.
2.2.2. Viewing Comprehension and Audiovisual Input
Viewing comprehension has been a focus of SLA research for several decades. Virtually all studies indicate that on-screen text facilitates understanding of the video (Markham, 1989; Chung, 1999; Bianchi & Ciabattoni, 2008; and many others). Although it has often been suggested that AV input might hinder learning by increasing the cognitive load (Chandler & Sweller, 1991; Sweller, 1994; Taylor, 2005), captions can also be seen as reducing the cognitive load, as they facilitate parsing. For instance, studies indicate lower proficiency learners tend to show high reliability on screen text (Bianchi & Ciabattoni, 2008; Bairstow & Lavaur, 2011; Lavaur & Bairstow, 2011). In this way, captions offer additional support, assisting learners in following the narrative and reducing the cognitive load put into decoding spoken language, thereby reducing cognitive load. Given that the study focuses exclusively on beginner learners, the following sections presents results found for this level group. 
First, Markham (1989) compared the effects of captioned versus non-captioned viewing for beginner, intermediate, and advanced university students (n = 31).  The videos used in the experiment consisted of two excerpts from an educational program, lasting 2.5 and 4.5 minutes each. Following each viewing, participants completed a multiple-choice test: twelve items for the first excerpt and sixteen items for the second. Results indicated that participants at all proficiency levels benefited more from captioned than non-captioned viewing. This suggests that viewing comprehension was enhanced by captions and that the presence of on-screen text did not impede comprehension in beginner learners. 
Bianchi and Ciabattoni (2008) aimed to compare the effect of subtitles, captions and no on-screen text among three different proficiency levels (beginner, intermediate and advanced adult learners). 85 students aged between 18 and 45 years old, all native Italian speakers, watched a series of clips from the movies Fantasia and Harry Potter under these three different conditions. Their comprehension was tested with a series of multiple-choice questions after each clip. The questions were aimed to test their comprehension in terms of content, vocabulary, and use of lexico-grammatical structures. Results showed that participants with low proficiency level benefited most from subtitles in both types of films, outperforming both captions and control groups. However, in case of the movie Harry Potter, the difference between the subtitled and the captioned condition was not significant, suggesting that with familiar and visually rich input, beginner students relied both on text and visual information. Overall, researchers concluded that ‘greater semantic match between audio-video-text input helped achieve higher results at all levels of proficiency in short-term comprehension task’ (Bianchi & Ciabattoni, 2008, p. 87).
Lavaur and Bairstow (2011) further investigated beginner learners' reliance on visual versus text information by differentiating between comprehension of information present in the dialogue and in the image. 30 French native speakers aged 15-18 years old with low proficiency level in English were exposed to a movie extract in English under three conditions: original version without subtitles, original version with English captions, and original version with French subtitles. A comprehension questionnaire administered after the viewing included 21 questions related to images and 21 questions related to dialogues. It was revealed that the uncaptioned condition benefitted images comprehension the most, followed by English captions and French subtitles. As far as the dialogue information is concerned, the order of conditions was reversed: participants provided with French subtitles achieved the highest comprehension scores, followed by participants exposed to English captions and uncaptioned input. Researchers suggest that in case of low proficiency learners, visual information is best processed when no on-screen text is present. However, the opposite is true for dialogue: L1 subtitles enhance dialogue comprehension and decrease image reliance. Such results predict that beginners rely mostly on subtitles for comprehension at the expense of visual information. 
Finally, a series of studies conducted by Bairstow and Lavaur (2011) also confirmed a facilitating role of on-screen text for beginner learners. In the first study, 32 French native speakers were divided into four groups according to their proficiency level (two high- and two low-proficiency groups) and the experimental conditions (original version vs. subtitles). Participants were exposed to an 8-minute fragment of North by Northwest movie by A. Hitchcock. Their comprehension was assessed after viewing via a questionnaire comprising 45 questions covering three types of information: visual, audio, and situational data. Results revealed that beginner learners achieved significantly higher comprehension scores with subtitled videos compared to non-subtitled ones. This suggests that they strongly rely on text for understanding both visual and audio information. The second study used the same movie segment, but with a wider group of participants at three proficiency levels, (advanced, intermediate, and beginner) and with three viewing conditions: original version, captioned version and subtitles. This time, the comprehension questionnaire was comprised of 42 questions concerning visual and dialogue information. The analysis of general comprehension scores showed a significant interaction between film version and participants’ proficiency in English: while the advanced learners benefitted more from original version, beginners’ comprehension scores significantly increased with subtitles. There was no significant effect of on-screen text for the intermediate group.[3.1] Thus, the three studies involving beginner en route adult learners showed the reliance on subtitles and/or captions with less experienced learners that seems to decrease at high proficiency levels. 
As far as FE studies as concerned, very few studies so far explored comprehension at the most initial stages of acquisition. The only study that used AV input to test comprehension was conducted by Han & Sun (2013), in which English and Japanese native speakers were exposed to 10 short videos in Chinese and performed a series of test. The second test focused on viewing comprehension and required the participants to generally describe what the episode was about. Results revealed that, overall, both English and Japanese students achieved a mean of around 60% for viewing comprehension. On the other hand, in a previous study on reading by Han & Peverly (2007), participants exposed to a text in Norwegian achieved lower scores (around 16%). These results might suggest that the presence of visual information in AV input facilitated comprehension for FE learners. 
In summary, existing research on comprehension from AV input indicates that beginner learners rely heavily on on-screen text. Although textual support may reduce attention to other information (Lavaur & Bairstow, 2011), it improves overall comprehension at the beginner level and is not perceived as interfering with participants’ understanding of the video (Chung, 1999). Beginners may not yet have developed sufficient listening skills to rely solely on aural information and thus require additional support. However, previous studies suggest that this reliance diminishes as proficiency increases, with advanced learners benefiting more from original or captioned videos than from subtitled input. 
Given the critical importance of on-screen text in compensating for developing listening skills, it is necessary to distinguish between the various types of textual support, as they impose different cognitive demands and offer distinct pedagogical advantages. The following section reviews the role of different types of on-screen text and analyses how each modulates cognitive load and influences the acquisition process for beginner language learners.
2.2.3. The Role of On-Screen Text
A central debate in the literature regarding on-screen text concerns the distinction between interlingual subtitles (providing a translation in the learner's L1) and intralingual captions (providing the text in the target language). These two modalities place fundamentally different demands on the learner's cognitive architecture and have been widely explored in previous SLA literature. These two modalities of on-screen text are often contrasted with original audio with no textual support, or the non-captioned condition. 
For instance, Liao et al. (2020) investigated the effects of four viewing conditions—interlingual, intralingual, bilingual, and no subtitles—on the cognitive load, visual attention, and comprehension of native Chinese university students watching English-language videos. Using eye-tracking, self-reported cognitive load assessments, and free recall exercises, the study found that participants devoted significantly more visual attention to bilingual subtitles than to L1 subtitles, with no significant difference observed between English and bilingual conditions. Regarding cognitive load, the "no subtitles" condition was perceived as the most demanding, while the bilingual condition was reported as the least, a finding the authors attribute to the redundancy of information across both languages. Crucially, comprehension scores did not differ significantly across any of the four conditions, suggesting that while bilingual subtitles do not induce cognitive overload, they provide no measurable cognitive benefit. The researchers conclude that the participants' visual attention toward the subtitles is likely driven by viewing habits and the dynamic nature of the text rather than the functional utility of the information
Overall, meta-analyses conducted on incidental vocabulary learning from AV input studies indicated a positive effect of captioning on vocabulary acquisition (i.e., Montero-Pérez et al., 2013; Kurokawa et al., 2024). While it has been usually proven beneficial for advanced learners (Markham, 1999; Winke et al., 2010) and for intermediate learners (Baltova, 1999; Montero Perez et al., 2014; Baranowska, 2020), its role seems to be more complex in the case of low proficiency learners. In fact, Kurokawa et al. (2024) suggest that beginner and low-intermediate learners’ difficulties in learning from captioned input might be due to their limited attentional capacity, as well as their insufficient ability to read the captions. Moreover, Vanderplank (1988) reported that AV input seemed to have enhanced learning mostly in high rather than low proficiency students, since “at beginner and low-intermediate levels, the language may not be comprehensible enough, even with textual support” (Vanderplank, 1988, p. 9). Due to the fact that the thesis focuses on AV input processing at FE, the following sections shed light on studies that examined language learning from AV input with beginner and novice adult learners exclusively. 
2.2.4. AV Input and Novice Learners
2.2.4.1. Beginner Learners
Sydorenko (2010) examined the effect of AV input on four different aspects of acquisition (learning of written and aural word forms, vocabulary gains, attention to input and vocabulary learning strategies) on twenty-six beginning adult learners of L2 Russian. The participants were randomly divided into three stimulus conditions: video with audio and captions (VAC), video with audio (VA) or video with captions (VC). The materials were two- or three-minutes long extracts from a Russian comedy series for native speakers, followed by a series of tests. The results revealed that, overall, the participants benefited from the input in different ways. For instance, VAC and VC groups scored higher at written form recognition, while the VA group performed better at aural word recognition. In addition, the VAC group performed better at meaning recognition than the VA group. Interestingly, the researcher found that participants paid the most attention to captions, then to video and finally to the audio. As far as practical application of the study is concerned, Sydorenko (2010) concludes that different types of input should be used to enhance learning of different skills or systems, for instance, captions to acquire vocabulary and no textual support to improve listening skills. 
Secondly, D’Ydewalle and Pavakanun (1995) analysed two populations (adults in experiment 1 and adolescent in experiment 2, all with beginner learners of English) in two studies with a. In Experiment 1, participants (n = 90) watched a 12-minute-long cartoon in English or Dutch soundtrack and/or subtitles) to then perform a sentence construction and a meaning recognition test. The results showed that there was a significant main effect of subtitling on the vocabulary test, yet not on the sentence construction. Dubbing appeared to have no significant effect on either one of the tests. In experiment 2 with the same methodology but with adolescents (99 grade 9 students), no such difference was observed. In addition, while the adults outperformed the adolescent learners on the meaning recognition test, the later scored higher at sentence construction. Thus, in this case the age of participants could have played a role in addition to their language level. 
Finally, Raine (2012) investigated the effect of viewing authentic subtitled videos on thirty-nine adult Japanese beginner learners of English under four conditions: English video with no subtitles, with intralingual or with standard subtitling and with bilingual subtitles. Participants completed a pre- and a post-test after watching a 6-minute-long video twice. The findings showed no significant difference between conditions. However, bilingual subtitling appeared to be the most beneficial condition for the students[PO4.1]. 
Ultimately, the research indicates that there is no single "best" AV condition for beginner learners, as the effectiveness of any support modality is fundamentally tied to the specific learning objective. While textual support like intralingual or bilingual subtitles acts as a vital scaffold that lowers cognitive load and aids vocabulary and meaning recognition, it can simultaneously divert the learner's attention away from the aural input.
Despite a rather considerable number of studies of language learning from AV input in en route learners, very few studies have focused on ab initio learners. Given that research has demonstrated learners' capacity to process and acquire linguistic skills from minimal classroom exposure (Hendriks & Prodeau, 1999; Rast, 2008; Ellis & Sagarra, 2010; and many others), it is highly probable that they are also capable of breaking into a new language through AV input. Thus, the following discusses all the previously conducted FE studies with AV input and their implications for the research. 
2.2.4.2. Novice Learners
The first FE study that used AV input to investigate initial L2 incidental vocabulary learning was conducted by Gullberg et al. (2010, 2012), who exposed Dutch native speakers to 7) of a weather report in Chinese. Input was specially tailored for the study, and the participants had no previous knowledge of Chinese or any language typologically and genetically similar to the TL. The target words were controlled for their position in the utterance and for their frequency. Additionally, half of the TWs were highlighted with gestures that were scripted and controlled by frame-by-frame analysis of digital video. Participants were tested on their development of segmental, phonotactic and lexical knowledge of Mandarin Chinese in a test battery. The word recognition showed a clear bias towards frequent words (eight occurrences) and towards disyllabic words, as opposed to monosyllabic items. Frequent words were also easier to segment. These results underscore the crucial role of frequency of occurrence of words in AV input, similarly to what was found in classroom settings (Rast, 2008). However, there was no significant difference between the amount of exposure (7 or 14 minutes). 
To test participants’ ability in mapping meaning to sound strings, an auditory picture-word matching task was conducted. Results revealed that Dutch native speakers were capable of mapping meaning to disyllabic words and that gestural highlight enhanced their performance. As far as extracting regularities and generalising is concerned, the participants were tested on their ability to detect syllable structure violations in the TL. Surprisingly, participants were effective at finding structural syllabic violations even in the case of TL items not included in the stimuli. Moreover, the higher the mount of exposure, the more sensitive they were to these features. 
In sum, results proved that ‘adult learning mechanism is more powerful than is normally assumed when faced with small amounts of complex, continuous AV language input’ (Gullberg et al., 2010, p. 1). The studies demonstrate the remarkable efficacy of the adult implicit learning mechanism when processing rich, AV input, proving that adult learners can rapidly segment speech and map meaning to new words, with performance significantly aided by high word frequency, disyllabic structure, and gestural cues. In summary, these findings challenge the belief that adults are slow to acquire new language structures; instead, research shows that they are capable of sophisticated, fast linguistic extraction and pattern generalization when provided with authentic AV input.
Partial replication of Gullberg et al. (2012) by Malessa and Lim (2016) with Urdu as TL showed different results. In their study, 38 English native speakers with no previous knowledge of the TL were exposed to a 4-minute weather report in Urdu (with strictly controlled language and highlights). As opposed to Gullberg et al. (2012), the participants were asked to view the material at least twice within one week. Then, they completed two tasks: an oral word recognition task and a meaning recognition task (there were 24 target words and 24 fillers in both tasks). Three input factors were taken into account in data analysis: item frequency (with frequent items appearing five or more times), gestural highlights and loanwords from the L1. Results showed that the participants correctly identified 55.24% of the items in the word recognition task and 60.95% of items in the meaning recognition task, which was considered just above chance. The effect of frequency and gestural highlights on acquisition was inconclusive; however, the loanwords had a recognition success of over 85%. The authors conclude that their study did not show acquisition in adults at FE to AV input (as opposed to Gullberg et al., 2012), although it showed that item transparency enhances learning at initial stages in line with Shoemaker and Rast (2013). 
Bisson et al. (2014) adapted a slightly different approach towards AV input processing and learning at FE.  The researchers exposed English native speakers (n = 64) to AV input (four episodes of the movie SpongeBob Square Pants, 25 minutes long) in three subtitling conditions: standard subtitling (L2 sound with L1 subtitles), reversed subtitling (L1 sound with L2 subtitling) and intralingual subtitling (both sound and subtitles in L2). A control group with L1 audio with no subtitles was included. The L2 was Dutch, and the participants had no previous knowledge of it. The two main aims of the study were to explore to what extent adults process different subtitling conditions using eye-tracking and to test incidental vocabulary acquisition under the three conditions. It was observed that participants read the subtitles in all three conditions. The difference between average fixation duration in the subtitle area was not significant between any of the three conditions. However, Tukey post hoc analyses confirmed that the three experimental groups spent significantly more time fixating in the subtitle area than the control group which watched the video without subtitles, proving that their fixation times were due to the reading and not to the visual features of the movie. These findings suggest that ab initio learners tend to read the subtitles independently of the subtitling condition. The researchers suggest it is due to various features of the input (orthographic similarities between Dutch and English, subtitles’ saliency, redundancy of information of the image) or simply the dynamic character of subtitles that attracts attention. As far as incidental vocabulary acquisition is concerned, there were no significant differences between the groups in the unexpected auditory vocabulary test and there was no main effect of subtitling condition on the test score. Thus, it appeared that vocabulary acquisition did not take place, which can be attributed to a short exposure time (25 minutes) and/or the fact that the test only measured recognition and participants might not have been able to reach recognition level (Bisson et al., 2014). 
Han and Liu (2013) compared the initial processing of Chinese AV input by 20 Japanese and English native speakers through a procedure of repeated video exposure followed by a battery of tests, including free recall, comprehension, note-taking, elicited imitation, and working memory. Results showed that while English speakers generally outperformed Japanese speakers across most tasks, differences were significant specifically in elicited imitation and working memory; additionally, a positive correlation was observed between free recall and note-taking, alongside a negative correlation between elicited imitation and working memory. The researchers concluded that participants only "scratched the surface" of the input, a finding consistent with previous studies. Despite achieving over 60% comprehension, note-taking data indicated that learners relied heavily on top-down processing strategies utilizing prior knowledge and visual images rather than bottom-up code-breaking. The authors linked these results to the Shallow Processing Hypothesis (Clahsen & Felser, 2006), which suggests that, unlike children, adult learners rely primarily on top-down processing, which may explain their less successful ultimate attainment compared to L1 learners.
Importantly, all previously discussed studies focused uniquely on incidental acquisition. Virtually the first FE study to employ an intentional learning design was conducted by Miralpeix et al. (2024). Their participants, 46 native Catalan/Spanish speakers who all knew English as L2 with no previous knowledge of Polis, were exposed to a 3-minute advert with audio in English (known, L2) with Polish (novel, TL) subtitles. Although the participants were familiar with the language of the audio, the subtitles were presented to them in a completely unfamiliar language. After watching the advert twice, participants completed a timed meaning recognition test with 15 TWs. It is important to mention that even though they were instructed to try to learn as much as possible from the new language (without specifying the language it was), they were not informed about the test following the viewing. In addition, participants completed a linguistic aptitude test LLAMA B (Meara, 2019) in order to explore possible aptitude effects on the results. 
Results showed that the average score of the meaning recognition test was of 7.72 out of 15 points and the results ranged from as low as two points to almost maximum (14 points). In addition, there were 10 test items whose meaning was correctly identified by more than 50% of the participants. The statistical analysis showed a significant correlation between learning and word frequency (r = .575, p = .025). Thus, similarly to Gullberg et al. (2010, 2012), frequency appeared to play a role with even a minimal (3, 7 or 14 minutes) exposure to AV input. As far as linguistic aptitude is concerned, the results showed a significant correlation between the meaning recognition test and LLAMA B test’s results. In fact, aptitude scores explained up to 24.3% of vocabulary test scores. In addition, it was found that aptitude assisted meaning recognition of the five most difficult words. In sum, the study contributed to the field of FE research in numerous ways. Firstly, it confirmed that adult learners are able to extract some lexical knowledge from AV input even after a minimal exposure, reinforcing Gullberg et al. (2010, 2012) findings. Additionally, we must keep in mind that the TL was provided just in the on-screen text (contrary to Gullberg et al., 2010, 2012, where it was provided just in the audio), so it proves that learners also benefit from textual support. Secondly, the participants were explicitly asked to focus on the new language and try to understand as much as they could. Attracting their attention to the language could have been a considerable booster to acquisition, as Bisson et al. (2014) suggested. Finally, as far as the learner-related variable is concerned, the study showed that linguistic aptitude assists acquisition not only in en route learners (Doughty, 2019), but also in novice learners. The researchers note, however, that aptitude test tapped on vocabulary learning, which might have favoured a positive correlation between the two variables. 
Two partial replications of Miralpeix et al. (2023, 2024) with different languages corroborated their findings. Olender (2021)  used the same 3-minute advert, this time with subtitles in Spanish, to investigate initial processing of Polish native speakers (n = 20). In other words, the L1 and the TL were reversed with regards to Miralpeix et al. (2024). Participants had no knowledge of Spanish (some participants had a beginner level of typologically similar French, yet there was no significant difference between their scores and the rest of the experimental group). The participants also completed the LLAMA B (Meara, 2019) test for linguistic aptitude and 15 items meaning recognition test. The TWs were adapted to avoid words that could be known to the participants due to the popularity of Spanish language abroad (i.e. music, series, travels, etc.). Before viewing the video twice, participants were also instructed to focus on the new language and try learning as much as they could of it. Results revealed that Polish native speakers were able to correctly recognize the meaning of 12.1 TWs on average, ranging from 5 to 15 points. Thus, they scored significantly higher than the control group (M = 7.4). Comparing with Miralpeix et al. (2024), it is possible that Spanish was simply easier to process than Polish at FE. Interestingly, it was found that frequency did not affect acquisition, but relevance did (r = .606, p = .017). No significant correlation was found between linguistic aptitude score and meaning recognition scores. However, it is notable that the participants scored lower on the LLAMA B test than participants in the original study (M = 5.8 vs. M = 10.28). Consequently, it is possible that when the task is less challenging and aptitude level is low, aptitude does not play a role in learning at FE.  
In another partial replication, Werkman (2022) used the same advert to expose Dutch native speakers (n = 30) to Romanian subtitles (with audio in English). Once again, two input-related variables (frequency and relevance) and one learner-related variable (linguistic aptitude) were analysed. Participants had no previous knowledge of Romanian, and a control group of 12 Dutch speakers was included. The participants completed two linguistic aptitude tests (LLAMA B and LLAMA D, Meara, 2019) and after watching the advert twice (with instructions to learn as much as possible from the new language as well), they completed the meaning recognition test comprised of 15 TWs (the items were also slightly adapted to the needs of the study). The results showed that the participants achieved an average score (M = 8.3) ranging from 2 to 14 points in the test.  The control group scored significantly lower (M = 3.75). As for the input features, both frequency and relevance correlated significantly to test scores. However, linguistic aptitude was not significantly correlated to vocabulary learning, but the mean was rather low for both tests (M = 4.3 for LLAMA B, M = 8.13 for LLAMA D). 
In sum, the replications corroborated the findings of the original study (Miralpeix et al., 2023, 2024) showing that adults learners are able to make meaning-form connections when exposed for the first time to minimal AV input with on-screen text in a completely novel language. The studies added more in-depth findings on initial learning, as well as on input and learner factors. First, it might be that the amount of learning simply depends on the L1 vs TL combination. For instance, Spanish seems to be easier to learn for Polish speakers than Polish is for Spanish NSs. Additionally, while word relevance seems to enhance learning, the impact of frequency on initial learning from AV input seems to be relevant only in more challenging conditions. Finally, considering the debate on whether linguistic aptitude assists the process of breaking into a new language, it seems that this variable only has an impact on learning when the task is challenging enough. This may explain why in Miralpeix et al. (2023, 2024) the aptitude explained up to 24% of vocabulary test result. However, the aptitude score must also be high enough to impact learning, which explain why no correlation was found between aptitude and learning in Olender (2021) and in Werkman (2022).
Having established that the adult brain is capable of pattern detection and rapid processing and learning during the initial stages of exposure to AV input, a critical question arises: what accounts for the variance in these outcomes? While FE previous studies provide insight into the how of initial processing, they also reveal that linguistic gain is rarely uniform. This variability suggests that successful acquisition in AV contexts is not merely a product of exposure, but the result of an interaction between a series of factors. In line with the analytical framework of this thesis, these are divided into input-related factors (the objective characteristics of the stimulus) and learner-related factors (the internal cognitive and linguistic profile of the individual).
2.3. Factors Influencing Vocabulary Acquisition at FE
Contemporary SLA research recognizes vocabulary as a fundamental cornerstone of language acquisition. Teng (2021) starts his book on vocabulary learning from captioned videos stating that “vocabulary is the foundation for learning a language. Without sufficient vocabulary, claiming sufficiency is impossible” (p. 1). Similarly, Schmitt et al. (2015) state that vocabulary knowledge is fundamental to all language use. In fact, research has consistently proven the crucial role of vocabulary in L2 proficiency. For example, Seashore and Eckerson (1940) already suggested a relationship between vocabulary size and a series of skills, such as reading, or comprehension. In addition, Meara (1996) claimed that L2 learners with bigger vocabulary sizes showed to be more proficient in a variety of skills (all the rest being equal) than those with smaller vocabularies, and that vocabulary size plays a major role in almost all aspects of L2 proficiency. Miralpeix and Muñoz (2018) investigated the relationship between receptive vocabulary size of upper-intermediate/advanced English learners and the four linguistic skills (reading, writing, listening and speaking) and found that receptive vocabulary size explained results obtained in all four skills to a large extent. For this reason, there is no doubt that vocabulary knowledge is essential in SLA. 
However, before discussing vocabulary acquisition more in depth, it is crucial to understand what it means to ‘know a word’. As Carroll (2013) points out, “words are not primitives of language but complex objects, consisting of a variety of different representations” (Carroll, 2013, p. 2). In other words, any lexical entry in our mental lexicon contains a number of features, which can be classified as semantic (main and secondary meanings, pragmatics, etc.) and formal features (morphology, orthography, auditory/articulatory shape, etc.). Ellis (1994) summarises that in order to know a word, learners’ input lexicon must categorise features such as a novel sound pattern, a new orthographic pattern, pronunciation, orthographic sequence pattern, its syntactic properties, its place in lexical structure, its conceptual underpinnings and, finally, map these features to the semantic and conceptual meanings creating a form-meaning link. Hence, our understanding of a vocabulary item is complex and involves many levels of knowledge that allow us to use the item correctly. 
Secondly, given the complex nature of words themselves, there are various approaches to explain how words are processed. Carroll (2013) presents four features of lexical learning which contribute to the most common ways of analysing vocabulary processing and acquisition in FE studies: 
a)	Computation (computing/structuring in real time) vs. storage (retrieving from memory): as ‘words are arbitrary and conventional associations of sounds and meaning’ (Carroll, 2013, p. 2), they cannot be computed in real time but retrieved from memory and acquired via input.  Consequently, it is believed by some linguists that the lexicon is composed of items that can appear separated from any linguistic context, for instance, in a word list (DiSciullo & Williams, 1987). As far as storage is concerned, although the mainstream hypothesis is that words are stored while sentences are not (Aitchison, 2003), research shows that the issue is more complex. Marcus et al. (1992) proved that in grammar acquisition by English native speakers, while regular elements such as past tense morphemes are computed in real time, storage was observed for irregular items which cannot be computed in real time. The same was observed in research with speakers of other languages (Golato, 2006). On the other hand, Baayen et al. (2002) showed that some highly frequent regular grammar structures seemed to be stored. Similarly, Wray (2002) presented evidence of L2 learners storing entire strings of speech, for instance, sentences or formulas. 
b)	Words and meanings: word meaning is undoubtedly an essential element of word knowledge that allows learners to produce meaningful output in L2. VanPatten (1996) argues that in the very first stages of acquisition learners focus exclusively on meaningful elements of linguistic input. However, this hypothesis was not confirmed either by Han and Peverly (2007) or by Han and Park (2008), who showed that novice learners focus mostly on highly frequent items and struggled with meaning recognition when no extra linguistic cues were provided. FE studies such as Carroll (2012) and Gullberg et al. (2010) proved that ab initio learners were able to map words to their pictorial representations. However, it is not clear whether participants were able to develop meaning representations of these items. In other words, the ability to match sound to the picture associated with it in the experiment does not provide us with any evidence of a target-like semantic representation that learners should develop. 
c)	Words and sounds: it has been shown that people are naturally able to segment words from speech stream in both artificial languages (Saffran et al., 1996; Folia et al., 2010) and natural unknown L2 (Gullberg et al., 2010, 2012; Shoemaker & Rast, 2013), which is generally defined as statistical learning. In psycholinguistics, segmentation is regarded as the first, pre-lexical stage of lexical activation that is based on the natural ability of detecting abstract regularities in the sound system (Cutler, 2008), i.e. tone, stress, accents, etc. In this case, the second stage would include connecting form to its semantic representation (word recognition). Statistical learning, however, does not seem to be the only factor in play when acquiring vocabulary at FE. For instance, in Gullberg et al. (2010) Dutch native speakers showed bias to reject one-syllable words, when these items are the most common in Chinese (TL): rather than on statistical learning, they must have relied on their L1. The same bias towards L1-related segmentation was shown in two more FE studies: Carroll (2012) and Rast and Dommergues (2003), where both reported the effect of transparency on comprehension and acquisition. 
d)	Words and grammar: some word aspects, such as gender or number, need encoding autonomous grammatical features (Carroll, 2013, p. 8). Research with en route learners has systematically confirmed that transfer can take place with these features if they resemble L1 words (Altenberg, 2002; Bongartz, 2002). However, up to now, very few FE studies further examined possible transfer of grammatical word features from L1. 
In sum, given the complexity of processing and encoding vocabulary and all the information the learner needs to possess to, indeed, know a word, L2 vocabulary processing is not yet fully understood and more research is needed. So far, FE research in lexical acquisition has approached word processing focusing on four starting points: storage, meaning acquisition, statistical learning, or transfer from the L1. However, beyond the scope of vocabulary knowledge, another central issue in current SLA literature is the distinction between intentional and incidental vocabulary learning. This dichotomy addresses whether lexical items are assimilated through explicit focus or through the implicit, incidental exposure characteristic of communicative interaction.
2.3.1. Incidental and Intentional Learning 
Gullberg et al. (2010) in their FE study on vocabulary acquisition from a short weather forecast in Chinese enquire “whether performance would improve if participants’ attention was drawn to specific aspects of the input in the instructions, turning the task into a more explicit learning experience” (p. 258). This question arises due to the fact that the study design tapped into participants’ incidental learning, as opposed to intentional learning. As Hulstijn (2001) describes it, the distinction between incidental and intentional learning “theoretically, [...] has become difficult to maintain; methodologically, the distinction is essential for any researcher intending to design a vocabulary experiment” (Hulstijn, 2001, p. 11). 
In the first place, it is important to state that the distinction between the two types of acquisition has been widely studied in both SLA and psychology under a variety of contrastive terms, such as implicit vs. explicit, incidental vs. intentional, unconscious, or subconscious vs. conscious, unattended vs. attended learning, etc. All these terms, however, are concerned with the same distinction between the two cognitive processes. Ellis (1994) defines implicit learning as the type of learning which “is coming to learn the underlying structure of a complex stimulus environment by a process which takes place naturally, simply and without conscious operations” and adds that “simple attention to the stimulus suffices for implicit learning mechanisms to induce statistical and systematic regularities in the input environment” (Ellis, 1994, p. 214). 
Explicit learning process, on the other hand, can be defined as “a more conscious function where the individual makes and tests hypotheses in a search of a structure” (Ellis, 1994, p. 214) via assimilation of rules or selective learning. In current applied linguistic research, incidental learning is used to refer to retention without conscious commitment or intention to learn or memorise an item or a form, for example a word or expression (i.e., ‘picking up’ a vocabulary item), as opposed to intentional learning that implies conscious commitment to memorise a word, especially when rehearsal techniques are used for this purpose (Hulstjin, 2013, p. 2). 
In simpler terms, incidental learning is sometimes referred to as a “by-product” (Huckin & Coady, 1999), a “side effect” (Gass, 1999) or a casual “picking-up” (Hulstijn, 2013; Rott, 1999) of new vocabulary. This operationalization of the concept allows to avoid the problematic issue of instruction that does not fully control learners’ mindset or commitment to learning. Nevertheless, the problem of defining whether and to what extent vocabulary learning processes are conscious or unconscious remains. In fact, two hypotheses related to vocabulary learning are put forward in SLA. The implicit vocabulary learning hypothesis (Skinner, 1957) was developed in the behaviourist tradition, and it posits that meanings of words are acquired totally unconsciously as a result of abstraction from repeated exposures in a range of contexts. On the other hand, the explicit vocabulary learning hypothesis holds that “there is some benefit to vocabulary acquisition from the learner noticing novel vocabulary, selectively attending to it, and using a variety of strategies to try to infer its meaning from the context” (Ellis, 1994, p. 219). In the following paragraphs, arguments for implicit and explicit nature of vocabulary acquisition are presented. 
According to the UG research tradition, in which Chomsky (1988) compares language acquisition to the skill of walking: “you don’t learn to do it; you don’t do it because you see other people doing it; you are designed to do it at a certain time” (Chomsky, 1988, p. 173-174). In these terms, vocabulary learning would be a process that happens unconsciously under various circumstances of exposure to a language (both L1 and L2). Indeed, a series of arguments advocate for vocabulary learning being an unconscious process. In L1 contexts, acquisition is primarily driven by frequency distribution and operates independently of academic intelligence (Taylor, 1959; Cromer, 1991). This implicit mechanism extends to L2 contexts, where extensive reading enhances vocabulary knowledge regardless of an individual's comprehension ability (Stanovich & Cunningham, 1992), suggesting that lexical items are retained through exposure without conscious intent (Krashen, 1989). Further support is found in priming research, which reveals that language is processed at the morphological level and that real words are privileged over non-words (Kirsner & Smith, 1974; Murrell & Morton, 1974). Finally, lexical decision processes are deeply influenced by orthographic regularity, where the time required to process written input depends on the frequency and plausibility of grapheme-phoneme mappings (Seidenberg et al., 1984; Taft, 1991). Because L2 vocabulary acquisition is accelerated when the target language shares alphabetic, sequential, or mapping conventions with the L1 (Carroll & Sapon, 1955; Ellis & Beaton, 1993; Ellis, 1994), it is evident that implicit learning is profoundly shaped by the learner’s familiarity with the input's structural patterns, a conclusion consistent with findings in spelling research (Krashen, 1989).
Similarly, there is some evidence of implicit learning in spoken word acquisition: phonological representations of unfamiliar words are difficult to process for individuals with deficient short-term memory (STM), both L1 and L2 vocabulary acquisition is predicted by phonological STM span, short and plausible non-words are easier to process and L2 phonotactics familiarity to the L1 predicts learnability (Ellis, 1994). In fact, in a meta-analysis study of incidental vocabulary learning from different meaning-focused input types, Webb et al. (2023) found that for immediate and delayed post-tests, the mean proportions of target words learned was 17% and 15% for reading, 15% and 13% for listening, 13% and 17% for reading while listening, and slightly lower means were revealed for viewing condition, with 7% in immediate and 5% in delayed post-test. Lower scores for viewing conditions are explained by learners’ familiarity with reading and listening but not viewing, difficulties in manipulating the AV input for specific learners’ needs, and the fact that many AV materials have L1 speakers as target public and are not always adapted to the L2 learners’ level. All arguments considered, it is important to note that these findings suggest that novice learners exposed to a FL for the first time come to the task with a certain set of capacities coming from their implicit L1 memory developed by their L1 reading, spelling and word recognition skills. Thus, the closer the FL is to the L1 (e.g. spelling patterns, grapheme-morpheme mapping, phonotactics similarities) the more prepared the learners are to process the input.  
On the other hand, there is some evidence on the intentional character of vocabulary acquisition. Firstly, research indicates a high correlation between academic intelligence and reading comprehension skills and vocabulary size in adults, showing that while form processing is ‘insensitive’ to academic intelligence, meaning processing requires explicit learning and memory abilities (Ellis, 1994). In addition, it has been shown that conscious and intentional analysis and rehearsal of vocabulary have a beneficial effect on acquisition. Craik and Lockhart (1972) put forward the concept of ‘depth of processing’, according to which the storage in long- or short-term memory did not depend on the time or amount of exposure, but rather on the ‘depth’ with which a word is processed. Similarly, Laufer and Hulstijn (2001) introduced a notion of involvement, consisting of motivational (i.e. the need to understand the meaning) and cognitive (i.e. the search and the evaluation of a word) component that enhanced processing and thus acquisition of vocabulary. Additionally, research has demonstrated that deep processing through mediational strategies is highly effective in vocabulary learning, especially when metalinguistic strategies for inferencing and remembering are applied by learners (Rubin, 1981; Cohen & Aphek, 1981; O’Malley et al., 1985, and many others). 
Webb et al.’s (2020) meta-analysis of studies examining intentional vocabulary learning revealed that the mean proportion of target words retained in intentional learning conditions was of 39.4%, as opposed to the global mean proportion of less than 20% in incidental vocabulary learning meta-analysis study (Webb et al., 2023). In sum, nowadays researchers agree that independently of incidental or intentional learning situations, the more elaborate and the deeper the processing, the more successful the meaning acquisition (Hulstijn, 2001), although most studies indicate an advantage of intentional learning. 
When discussing implicit or explicit character of SLA, it is important to mention Schmidt’s (1990, 1993, 2001) noticing hypothesis which, according to Leow (2013), is “one of the most influential theoretical underpinnings in SLA (...) and has contributed tremendously to the centralization of both the roles of attention and awareness (...) in SLA” (p. 11). Defined as “the registration of the occurrence of a stimulus event in conscious awareness and its subsequent storage in long-term memory” (Schmidt, 1994, p. 166), noticing theory does not allow space for implicit learning, as its underlying tenet is that intake does not take place without awareness. There is, however, a distinction between lower and higher levels of awareness in Schmidt’s hypothesis: awareness at the level of noticing and awareness at the level of understanding, which implies a deeper processing and thus, more effective learning. 
Despite the fact than many initial studies conducted to confirm or discharge the hypothesis show the key role of awareness for learning (Leow, 1997, 2000, and many others), a vast body of research that used similar methodologies advocated for implicit learning taking place despite lack of attention or awareness (Williams, 2004, 2005; Leung & Williams, 2011, 2012). Furthermore, Williams (2013) indicates that there is a distinction between awareness and attention, arguing that ‘just because something is attended to does not mean we are necessarily aware of it’ and that ‘the attentional system can orient to stimuli that are not necessarily perceived’ (p. 41). In consequence, she believes that while there is learning without awareness, no learning can take place without attention. 
As far as vocabulary learning and attention is concerned, Godfroid and Schmidtke (2013) measured non-words learning from reading through an unannounced post-test in L1 Dutch speakers with advanced English skills. Eye-tracker was used to explore the role of attention in incidental non-words acquisition. Results revealed that the longer fixations on target words predicted meaning recognition in the post-test, showing that attention could have played a role in incidental vocabulary acquisition. [6.1]However, awareness, as measured through verbal reports, also seemed to impact learning. 
Knowing a word implies a high number of abilities, such as categorising a novel sound pattern, a new orthographic pattern, its spelling, pronunciation, its semantic properties etc. Thus, different implicit or explicit processes are involved in acquisition. As far as productive and receptive skills are concerned, learning seems to rely on frequency, regularity and developing a certain automaticity through implicit learning. However, the semantic component seems to depend more on explicit processing. Ellis (1994) summarises this distinction in the following way:
To the extent that vocabulary acquisition is learning these [word forms and regularities] surface forms of language, then vocabulary acquisition is an implicitly acquired skill. [...] However, the function of words is meaning and reference. [...] To the extent that vocabulary acquisition is about meaning, it is an explicit learning process. (Ellis, 1994, p. 267-268)
McGeoch (1942, p. 304) argues that “it is impossible to firmly state whether participants in incidental learning conditions did not have any kind of motivation, self-instruction or, generally, a mindset to learn from the stimuli”. For this reason, some psychologists suggested focusing more on the relationship between instruction, stimulus and measures of retention (Eysenck, 1982). For instance, Bransford et al. (1979) point out that in order to accurately measure retention (whether in incidental or intentional learning condition), the stimuli and the instructions that participants receive should be compatible with the test they are exposed to afterwards. In other words, if participants are asked to rate a word from a list according to their pleasantness or to define the part of speech that they represent (Hyde & Jenkins, 1973), it can be predicted that in a recall task, the participants who focused on semantic features of words will perform better than those focusing on non-semantic features. This notion of transfer appropriateness (Bransford et al., 1979) explains why reducing the distinction between incidental and intentional learning to instruction type is problematic. Even in incidental learning conditions, participants might be set to learn. However, they do not know in advance what they are expected to focus on, so the skills they acquired might not transfer to the measure they are exposed to. This, however, does not mean that they did not try learning, as the instruction itself cannot completely manipulate students’ attention or mindset towards the stimuli. Barcroft (2004) even argues that classifying vocabulary learning “as purely incidental or purely intentional does not accurately represent real-world vocabulary learning” (Barcroft, 2004, p. 201).
	In practice, various strategies tend to be applied to tap into incidental or intentional learning. Sok (2014) distinguishes between three lines of research depending on the theoretical standpoint: the learner-oriented, the method-oriented and the pedagogy-oriented line. Learner-oriented research assumes that “during incidental learning, the learner’s attention is fixated on something other than word-learning” (Sok, 2014, p. 25), thus, the aim is to create research conditions in which learners’ attention is deliberately turned towards other activities, for instance, reading comprehension tasks (Chen & Truscott, 2010) or reading for pleasure (Waring & Takaki, 2003). 
From the point of view of method-oriented research, learning can be defined as incidental under conditions in which participants are not informed about the follow-up test beforehand. In other words, it is the lack of information about the evaluation of their performance that decides whether the learning can be classified as incidental. It differs from the learner-oriented approach because in this case the focus of learners’ attention is not considered important if the follow up evaluation information is being withheld. In fact, such usage of research methodology for incidental learning was very common in the 20th century experimental psychology studies (Hulstijn, 2001). In the field of behaviourist psychology (Ebbinghaus, 1964; Postman, 1964; Eysenck, 1982), participants were usually tested on their acquisition (for instance, from a word list) in two conditions that differed in previous instructions. In intentional learning conditions, participants were informed that they would be tested on the materials they were provided with, while in incidental learning conditions they were not informed about the test that followed. Thus, in simple terms, the distinction between incidental and intentional learning would consist in either telling or not telling the students that they would be tested afterwards (Hulstjin, 2001).
Nevertheless, such distinction poses a series of problems. Hulstijn (2001, 2003) suggests using the terms incidental and intentional in this line of research as technical rather than theoretical terms, avoiding the direct link to learners’ attention, given that such distinction is hard to maintain at the theoretical level and it is hard to evaluate learners’ attention allocation, which is why the construct of engagement, meaning “greater depth of processing; better, more intense quality of information processing; degree of elaboration; quality of attention; richness of encoding” (Laufer & Hulstijn, 2001, p. 12) should be considered. Thus, Laufer and Hulstijn (2001) put forward the Involvement Load Hypothesis in which ‘task-induced involvement’ is a “motivation-cognitive construct that explains and predicts the degree to which learners will learn and retain unknown words” and which predicts a positive effect of need, search, and evaluation on incidental L2 vocabulary learning. 
Finally, the pedagogy-oriented research focuses on classroom learning of vocabulary when the main focus is on meaning or communication. In other words, “incidental learning is seen to be the result of learning in classrooms where language is not the primary object of instruction” (Sok, 2014, p. 30). For instance, Both Wode (1999) and Coll (2002) studies showed learning of vocabulary that was not explicitly presented in the classroom, suggesting that it was ‘picked-up’ from the oral input provided by the teacher. 
As far as FE studies are concerned, up to now the researchers have conducted experiments concerning both incidental (Gullberg et al., 2010, 2012; Bisson et al., 2014) and intentional (Dommergues & Rast, 2003; Ellis & Sagarra, 2010; Rast, 2008; Rast, 2010; Shoemaker & Rast, 2013) vocabulary learning. In the case of Miralpeix et al. (2023), as well as their two replications (Olender, 2021; Werkman, 2022), participants were explicitly asked to try to understand and to learn as much as possible of the TL prompting intentional learning, even though participants were not informed that they would be tested or what the nature of the test would be. This was intended to account for a learning environment in which novice learners approach L2 input with an intentional goal to learn a new FL. Similarly, given that this thesis aims to reflect the ecological validity of extramural learning environments, an intentional learning framework was deemed the most appropriate choice for the experimental design.
In sum, learners’ approach to language learning, including their motivation, necessity, involvement or simply the awareness of upcoming testing, plays a role in the processing of input both in en route and in ab initio learners. However, it is not always easy to draw a line between intentional and incidental learning, as learners’ mindset and their individual approach toward input cannot be fully controlled. Therefore, to address this complexity among participants consciously utilizing AV input for L2 acquisition, this thesis adopts the methodological approach established by previous research (Miralpeix et al., 2024; Olender, 2021; Werkman, 2022) asking learners to learn as much as they could from the input, without prompting specific linguistic elements included in the follow up testing. 
While the distinction between intentional and incidental learning highlights the cognitive orientation of the learner, this dichotomy represents only one of many factors involved in the acquisition. The effectiveness of these learning processes—whether conscious or subconscious—is intrinsically tied to the nature of the input itself. The characteristics of that input determines the availability of information and the efficiency with which the cognitive system can perceive, segment, and encode it.
2.3.2. Input-Related Factors 
Input-related factors represent the external variables inherent in the linguistic and multimodal stimuli that facilitate or hinder the initial mapping of form to meaning. At the FE stage, these features are particularly decisive, as the learner lacks a pre-existing TL knowledge to compensate for infrequent or non-transparent input. Consequently, the characteristic of the input largely dictate which lexical items are processed and/or retained. Therefore, this subsection analyses the most influential input-driven variables in multimodal contexts.
With the acknowledgment of input as a central construct in SLA (Krashen, 1985; Schwartz & Sprouse, 1996; Gass, 1997), research has increasingly shifted toward investigating how specific input features influence the acquisition process. Therefore, the research in L2 vocabulary acquisition has consistently analysed input features in relation to vocabulary learning. Studies conducted in a wide range of conditions, varying in input types and proficiency levels, show conclusively that word-related factors such as frequency, relevance, concreteness, imageability, cognateness or orthographic neighbourhood density, tend to have an impact on vocabulary learning (Crossley & Salisbury, 2010; Crossley et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2018; Goriot et al., 2018; De Wilde et al.,2020; Peters & Webb, 2018; and others). A detailed description on what has been revealed so far on the role of frequency, relevance and other word-related factors is presented below. 
	2.3.1.1. Frequency of occurrence
	One of the most studied word-related factors in vocabulary acquisition is frequency of occurrence, that is, the number of times a word appears in the input the learner is exposed to. Research shows that learners tend to know more high-frequency words than low-frequency words (Brysbaert et al. 2018) and evidence for the frequency effect can be found both in the case of written and aural input. Most studies which explored reading show that frequency enhances vocabulary learning (Horst et al., 1998; Rott, 1999; Webb, 2007; Pellicer-Sánchez & Schmitt, 2010; DeWilde et al., 2020), with a minimal encounter number between 8 and 10 times, depending on whether receptive or productive vocabulary knowledge is measured (Webb, 2007; Pellicer-Sánchez & Schmitt, 2010; Peters & Webb, 2018). It is also believed the high frequency in written text reduces reading time (Pellicer-Sánchez, 2016). On the other hand, in the case of listening, despite the number of studies being limited, there is some evidence showing the beneficial effect of word repetition on vocabulary acquisition, yet it appears that this effect is much smaller as compared to written input (Vidal, 2003, 2011). Therefore, word frequency in aural input does not seem to have the same effect on form recognition or meaning recall (van Zeeland & Schmitt, 2013). 
The effects of word frequency for language acquisition have also been analysed with AV input. Rodgers (2013) tested the effect of word frequency on vocabulary acquisition after exposing participants to over 7 hours (10 episodes) of TV programme with captions in English. It appears that frequency of occurrence affected acquisition only to some extent: a small positive correlation was found between frequency and the meaning recognition test, yet no correlation was found with the sensitive meaning recognition test scores. Another study by Peters et al. (2016) showed a positive correlation between frequency and learning gains after viewing a video, yet the researchers questioned the role of the frequency due to a significant correlation between frequency and participants’ vocabulary size. 
Peters and Webb (2018) explored the effect of frequency, cognateness and relevance on incidental learning after viewing one full-length TV programme to find that frequency had a significant positive relationship with vocabulary learning. In addition, the study showed that frequency seemed to have a bigger impact on meaning recall than on meaning recognition. Frequency was also shown to play a role in incidental acquisition of single words from AV input (Puimège & Peters, 2019) in adults who viewed a 30-minute-long reality show episode. Finally, Pujadas and Muñoz (2023) found an effect of frequency on word form learning from AV input in a study involving a group of 82 adolescents exposed to eight episodes of a 20-minute-long sitcom. 
In sum, the body of research regarding word frequency in AV contexts points toward a consistent facilitating role in lexical acquisition, especially in incidental learning setting. While early investigations yielded varying results, more recent studies have increasingly contributed to the assumption that frequency is a reliable predictor of learning gains. Notably, this effect is robust across both word form learning and meaning recall. These findings suggest that while the strength of the influence may vary, frequency serves as a fundamental mechanism for L2 vocabulary learning.	
Reflecting the established consensus on the significance of frequency in L2 vocabulary learning, this variable has been systematically explored across FE studies, resulting in substantive insights into its role in acquisition at the earliest stages. In classroom context, the VILLA project (Dimroth et al., 2013) found that in classroom setting frequency appeared to have an effect after 8 hours of exposure (Rast & Dommergues, 2003). However, Rast (2008) found no effect of frequency after 8 hours of exposure to instructed Polish course.  
Results from AV input-based FE studies indicate a more consistent role of frequency.  Gullberg et al. (2012) showed a frequency after only seven minutes of exposure to AV input. Their results revealed that recognition accuracy was higher for frequent items than for infrequent items and that only frequency and syllable features had a significant main effect on word recognition. In the case of mapping meaning to words, frequency interacted with gestural highlights to significantly affect the results. Researchers conclude that eight occurrences were enough to impact acquisition, differing from previous FE studies in classroom settings. 
Bisson et al. (2014) analysed the role of number of exposures to AV input (pictorial representations of meaning with written word and its oral representation) on incidental vocabulary learning at FE. Despite the significant difference between scores in translation recognition at two versus at eight exposures, there was no significant main effect of exposures number on learning. Researchers suggested that when form-meaning links can be created at first encounter, as in case of pictorial representations of concrete nouns’ meanings, further encounters do not have a relevant impact on acquisition. 
More recently, the role of frequency in vocabulary acquisition from AV input at FE was examined by Miralpeix et al. (2023). The results revealed that after only 6 minutes of exposure to a TV advert, there was a significant medium positive correlation between frequency of occurrence and learning gains. However, it should be taken into account that only four out of fifteen target items appeared more than once (twice, five or six times). The two partial replications of Miralpeix et al. (2023) present contrasting results. Olender (2021) found no correlation between frequency and learning after exposing Polish native speakers to the same advert in Spanish, and Werkman (2022) found a moderate significant correlation between frequency and meaning recognition scores. 
Therefore, it is notable that the research in language acquisition at FE has not yet provided conclusive results as far as the role of frequency is concerned. Although some researchers account for the role of frequency in statistical learning (Gullberg et al., 2012; Slobin, 1985) which is crucial when breaking into a new language, other studies show no correlation between the number of occurrences of lexical items in input and the ability to recognise the items after 6 minutes, 4 hours or even 6.5 hours of exposure. 
	In sum, although a vast body of evidence suggest an interaction between frequency of occurrence and vocabulary learning, researchers underline the need to explore the complexity of this factor in interaction with a series of other features (e.g., relevance, saliency, etc.) rather than trying to determine the frequency of exposure as an autonomous factor (Pellicer-Sánchez, 2020). 
	2.3.1.2. Relevance
	Another input factor to be accounted for when exploring learning from AV input is word relevance, conceptualized as the degree to which specific words are indispensable for achieving global comprehension. Research in experienced L2 learners has shown that lexical items that were crucial to answering comprehension question or, overall, to complete a task, were better retained than non-task relevant words (Peters, 2007; Peters et al., 2009). In listening and reading, Vidal (2003, 2010) found that the technical words (the words that were relevant to understand the input) were best retained when listening, and they were the second-best predictor of learning from aural input. However, this was not true for written input. As for AV input, Peters and Webb (2018) reported no significant contribution of word relevance in vocabulary learning from a TV programme. 
The few studies with en route learners showed limited results for the role of word relevance when learning in both classroom and AV input. However, the FE conditions differ considerably from the learners with some previous knowledge of the L2: when exposed to AV input in a completely new language, without the possibility to rely on previously acquired vocabulary, adults need to rely on some input features that help them to decode or to ‘break into’ a language. In the first place, frequency was considered as a potential benefit for learning, yet with differing results. For this reason, it is of great interest to consider whether the word relevance to understanding the input plays a significant role in the very first stages of language learning.
	In fact, three recent FE studies on AV input included relevance as a possible input factor affecting learning. Miralpeix et al. (2023) point out that the fact that some items that appeared only once were as successfully learnt as items that appeared six times in the six-minute exposure time suggest that word relevance can have an important role at FE. Olender (2021) in reversed languages conditions found a significant positive correlation between perceived word relevance and learning percentages, and a regression analysis showed significant contribution of relevance but not of frequency to the model. The analysis was repeated in the replication by Werkman (2022) with different languages set: a moderate significant correlation was found both between frequency and learning and relevance and learning, with relevance correlation to learning being slightly higher (r = .636) than the frequency correlation (r = .614). In conclusion, even though there is not much research in the role of relevance in ab initio learners, the first studies that approach the issue indicate a potential role of word relevance in language acquisition at FE For this reason, it is of interest for future research to include this input factor in the analysis. 
	2.3.1.3. Other Input Factors
	In addition to frequency and relevance, several other input features have been shown to play a significant role in how a new language is first processed. Factors such as cross-linguistic transparency, the position of a word within a sentence, and perceptual salience provide important cues that can help ab initio learners organize and understand novel information. These extra variables potentially act as a form of support, helping the learner to identify and decode new words during their first encounter with the language. 
Firstly, the impact of cognateness or transparency of input has been widely explored in previous SLA research. Although traditionally cognates were defined as historically related words with similar words that usually share their meaning (Ringbom, 2007), Peters (2020) suggest that for the needs of SLA research, cognates are any “words with a high form and meaning overlap regardless of the etymology” (p. 125). Dimroth et al. (2006) point out that the difference between the two terms - cognateness and transparency - lays in the focus on the input or on the learner. In other words, cognates are objectively classified as lexical items sharing characteristics between the L1 and the Ln, while transparency is based on learners’ psychotypology, so their individual perception of each lexical item. 
In en route learners, cognates were shown to be easier to process (Van Assche et al., 2013), to learn (Tonzar et al., 2009; de Groot & Keijzer, 2000, Ellis & Beaton, 1993), to retain (Rogers et al., 2015), and even to translate (Lemhöfer & Dijkstra, 2004; Costa et al., 2000; etc.) than non-cognates. In addition, Laufer and McLean (2016) demonstrated that the cognate effect is stronger in the low-proficiency than in high-proficiency learners, which suggests that the lack of language knowledge enhances relying on cognates. As far as ab initio learners are concerned, Rast and Dommergues (2003) reported a positive impact of transparency on sentence repetition task scores after 8 hours of FE to Polish. Based on analysis of word recognition at FE to Polish, Rast (2008) formulated ‘a continuum of lexical transparency’ (Rast, 2008, p. 94) describing how resemblance between lexical items in L1, Lns and the TL enhances recognition.
Similarly, Shoemaker and Rast (2013) found a significant effect of transparency of the target word with respect to the learners’ L1 in classroom settings at both testing times (0 and 6.5 hours of exposure), showing once again that the perceived transparency has an impact on learning a new language at FE. Interestingly, post-hoc analyses showed that the sensitivity to low transparency (LT) and high transparency (HT) developed differently over time: while the sensitivity to HT words did not seem to change between the two testing times, sensitivity to LT words increased significantly after 6.5 hours of exposure. However, the authors suggest that the stable nature of sensitivity to HT words might be due to a very high word recognition already at first testing time (88.4%), which did not leave much room for improvement at second testing time (95.9%). 
Moreover, Berthele (2011) exposed multilinguals to reading and listening in a new TL typologically similar to their L1s to test the potential effectiveness of multilingualism in developing receptive competences in a new FL. Participants performed tasks such as inferring meaning from context (word list), comprehension, and inferring meaning of cognates from a word list without any context. Results revealed that the number and the typology of languages had a significant effect only on the third task, thus, inferring the meaning of cognates without the context. In addition, knowing at least two languages typologically similar to the TL was shown to facilitate meaning recognition. Similar effect was found in Carroll (2014), where cognateness significantly enhances processing at FE: while beginner learners slightly outperformed novices, the latter still achieved an 89.3% recognition rate for German cognate names, underscoring the powerful facilitative role of cross-linguistic similarity even in the absence of prior language knowledge.
In conclusion, research has consistently proven the clear effects of cognateness or transparency at FE to a new language when learning in formal settings. However, very few studies so far analysed the role of cognateness when ab initio learners are exposed to AV input. Given previous research in en route learners, it could be hypothesized that transparency potentially affects language processing at FE to AV input. In fact, 50% of types in the input used in this study are cognates to one of participants' L2s (Spanish and Catalan). From the methodological perspective, such a high number of cognates in the input is essential, as it enables the participants to follow a 26-minute episode despite their lack of prior exposure to the TL. According to Hall's (2002) parasitic hypothesis of word learning, in the initial stages of learning participants tend to use their existing vocabulary knowledge to guess the meaning of new words. 
Nevertheless, we argue that for typologically similar languages, including cognates among target items does not demonstrate genuine vocabulary acquisition at FE, but rather the ability to guess based on prior knowledge (as shown in Berthele, 2011). The study on receptive multilingualism (the ability to communicate in your L1 with somebody with a different but similar L1 unknown to you) by Gooskens et al. (2018) showed that relying on cognates is a frequent strategy in language learning, as well as cognates guessing (see also Vanhove & Berthele, 2015): Spanish native speakers with minimal exposure to Italian achieved a score of 38.2% in the listening cloze test. For this reason, even though cognates or transparent lexical items in the study material unavoidably facilitate processing for Spanish/Catalan native speakers, they are not considered as evidence of acquisition. This is why no cognates are included among the target words in the study. There are, however, four false cognates that share their form with participants’ L1s (Spanish and Catalan), but they hold different meanings in the TL (Italian). These items are meant to control whether the participants acquired the meaning different to their L1s or whether they relied more on their L1 as suggested by the parasitic hypothesis (Hall, 2010). 
	Another word-related factor already examined in FE studies is item’s position in the utterance. Following the research on en route learners that shows a tendency to better process information in initial and in final sentence position (Klein, 1986; Barcroft & VanPatten, 1997), Rast and Dommergues (2003) found a significant effect of sentence position at both testing times (after 4 and 8 hours of exposure). Following these results, Rast and Shoemaker (2013) investigated the impact of word position after 0 and 6.5 hours of classroom exposure to find that the effect of position was significant at both testing times, concluding that these results “clearly point to a learner’s reliance on the edges of prosodic domains in the recognition of TL lexical items” (p. 175). In the context of AV input[PO7.1], the segmentation of text across subtitles lines can impact cognitive load; while line-initial and line-final positions are theoretically more salient, words placed at the 'exit point' (bottom-right) of a two-line subtitle are at a higher risk of being skipped due to the temporal constraints of the video (Bisson et al., 2014). 
In the third place, some researchers took into account the salience of input cues. As defined by Ellis (2016), salience refers to ‘the ability of a stimulus to stand out from the rest’ (Ellis, 2016, p. 342), which is determined by its psychophysical features (i.e. size, volume, etc.), our previous world experience and our expectations (i.e. the surprise element of something being different to what was predicted) (see also Gass, 1988; Rácz, 2013). Brown (1973) claimed that in L1 acquisition salience was more important than frequency of occurrence. In the case of L2, Goldschneider and DeKeyser (2001) showed that salience had the most significant impact on acquisition order. As far as vocabulary learning is concerned, several studies showed an interaction between L2 vocabulary learning and salience (e.g., Smith, 2003; Bruton et al., 2011; Ko, 2012; Elgort & Warren, 2014). 
Up to now, three FE studies considered the role of salience in acquisition from the earliest stages. Ellis and Sagarra (2010) compared novice learners' reliance on lexical and morphological cues at FE to Latin (laboratory settings) and Spanish (classroom settings) and hypothesised that the “learners' preference for lexical over morphological cues is affected by their salience [...], their reliability of interpretation [...] and their redundancy within the discourse context” (p. 87). In fact, their results revealed that both in short and in long-term, the learners tend to rely more on the lexical cues, and this can be attributed to their salience as previously suggested by the authors. 
In another FE study by Gullberg et al. (2012), the AV input (weather forecast in Chinese) was enhanced by strictly controlled gestural highlights which could facilitate comprehension. Thus, the role of gestural highlights in language acquisition was taken into account as an input factor. Results revealed no significant effect of gestural highlights in the word recognition task (as opposed to the significant effect of frequency and word length). However, in the sound-to-picture matching task, there was a significant interaction effect between frequency and gestural highlights. In conclusion, it seemed that the salience induced by gestural highlights played no role in isolation, but it did play a role in interaction with other input factors. 
Finally, McDonough and Trofimovich (2018) examined the role of salience in morphology acquisition of Esperanto structure. Although they did not find psychophysical salience effects determined by skewness of frequency in the input, they claim that the learner-related salience, which is, salience determined by learners’ previous experience and individual perception, could have affected processing at FE. These results were based on the relationship between languages spoken by the participants and their ease or difficulty in novel pattern acquisition. 
Despite such a wide array of input factors shown to influence L2 acquisition, the empirical evidence within FE contexts remains characterized by a lack of consensus. Although research into AV input has highlighted the specific roles of frequency and task relevance, the relative importance of these features varies significantly across studies, calling for further investigation into their precise impact at the earliest stages of learning.
Input, however, does not operate in isolation. Its effectiveness in the learning process is unavoidably mediated by the cognitive conditions of each learner. Therefore, to achieve a more holistic understanding of learning at FE, it is necessary to shift focus from the properties of the input to the learner factors that govern L2 processing and learning. The following sections explore how learner variables such as prior linguistic knowledge, language aptitude, and working memory interact and shape how a novel language is initially processed and learned.
2.3.3. Learner-Related Factors
Every language learner, either at beginner, intermediate or advanced level, brings to the table a set of individual differences (IDs) which play a role in its L2 linguistic development. Dörnyei (2006) states that “IDs are a prominent feature of SLA because a great deal of the variation in language learning outcomes is attributable, either directly or indirectly, to various learner characteristics” (p.1). Kidd et al. (2018) highlight that although linguists tended to underestimate the possibility of meaningful differences in language learning between individuals, nowadays “IDs are evident across all components of the linguistic system” and “understanding the contribution of these variables allows a better understanding of the mechanisms underlying human language” (Kidd et al., 2018, p. 1). As consequence, it is essential to include this “inconvenient truth” (Kidd et al., 2018, p. 1) in SLA research.
Overall, SLA theories present different approaches to IDs’ role in language processing and acquisition. For generativists, UG could only be subject to IDs under particular conditions, e.g.  neurocognitive disorders (van der Lely & Pinker, 2014). However, while these principles are stated for grammar, in the case of vocabulary IDs are expected to play a role given that “words are specific to individual languages and, therefore, must be learnt” (Kidd et al., 2018, p. 3). From an emergentist perspective, more importance has been given to input and learning mechanisms, including more variance caused by IDs. Recent research on the role of IDs in SLA usually reveals that learner-related variables affect acquisition, and they are large through linguistic development (Bates et al., 1995; Bornstein & Putnick, 2012), meaning that they usually play an important role in acquisition throughout time. What is more, they are proven to be in interaction with other factors, for example, with quality and quantity of received input through socioeconomic status (e.g. Hoff-Ginsberg, 1986; Hoff, 2003; Bradley et al., 2003 and many others), with cognitive variables such as working memory (e.g. Conti-Ramsden et al., 2001) and interrelated to executive function (e.g. Novick et al., 2014; Woodard et al., 2016; Nozari et al., 2016, and many others). 
Given the considerable impact of IDs on virtually every aspect of SLA, they can also be central to FE situations. In fact, some previous FE studies have already approached the interplay between IDs and initial learning (Singleton & Little, 1984; Hendriks & Prodeau, 1999; Lalleman, 1999; Park & Han, 2008; Rast, 2008; Ellis & Sagarra, 2010; Han & Liu, 2013; Dimroth et al., 2013; Miralpeix et al., in press; Olender, 2021; Werkman, 2022). In the following section, three IDs crucial to initial state investigation are discussed: previous linguistic knowledge, linguistic aptitude, and working memory. 
2.3.3.1. Previous Linguistic Knowledge 
The impact of L1 on L2 acquisition has been an object of SLA research for decades. Depending on tradition or approach to SLA theory, researchers have given a more or less prominent role to the L1 in the first stages of language learning. For instance, according to the generativists’ Full Transfer Hypothesis, the initial state of a L2 is the final state of the L1 (Schwartz & Sprouse, 1996). Among similar lines of research, Nation (2001) transmits the importance of previous linguistic experience included in the concept of learning burden, (the amount of effort required to learn a new word) which is strongly influenced by previous linguistic knowledge, as “the more the word represents patterns and knowledge that learners are already familiar with, the lighter is the burden [...] these patterns and this knowledge can be from the first language, from knowledge of other languages, and from previous knowledge of a second language” (p. 23). As Rast (2008) describes it, from this standpoint the L1 is “a filter through which the TL input passes” (Rast, 2008, p. 30). Given the extensive background of transfer research in SLA, the foundational role of the L1 in initial L2 processing remains a widely recognized and well-documented phenomenon.
The concept of language transfer can be traced back to Lado (1957) and Contrastive Analysis. Apart from putting forward the notions of positive transfer (an easy assimilation of L2 structures that correspond to L1 features) and a negative transfer or interference (a complication in L2 structures due to lack of equivalent or correspondent structures in the L1) Contrastive Analysis accounted for the fact that transfer is dependent not only on the native language, but on the native culture. This concept, however, was criticised by some researchers given its complete dependence on linguistic features and not on individual learners’ processing. For instance, Kellerman (1979) argues that transferability of linguistic structures does not depend on their existence in the L1, but rather on combination of the learner’s psychological structure of their L1, their knowledge of the TL and the perceived distance between the two languages. In this way, Kellerman (1979, 1995) attributes a crucial role in linguistic transfer to learners’ creativity and their perception of the language, for instance, psychotypology (similarity to an L1 perceived by the learner) and thus suggests that transferability can vary depending on the learner (Jarvis & Pavlenko, 2008). 
Apart from the L1, however, a learner may have knowledge of other languages, which is the case of bilingual and multilingual learners. Smith and Kellerman (1986) proposed the term of transfer as cross-linguistic influence (CLI), which is more inclusive and refers to the interaction between one language and all the other languages known to the learner (not just L1), independently on the order of acquisition. More recently, Jarvis and Pavlenko (2008) defined CLI “the influence of a person’s knowledge of one language on that person’s knowledge or use of another language” (p. 1). The inclusion of other languages, not only the L1 or the native language, was a relevant step for the SLA research, as it acclaimed that not only our first language, but any other Ln can have an influence on new languages acquisition (referred to as L3 in Muñoz, 2006, L2+ in Dimroth et al., 2006, etc.). In fact, Rast (2008) claims that research has been mainly focused on L2 acquisition without accounting for other languages known by learners, hence the problematic terminology referred to acquisition when more languages are in play, which also reflects the lack of full understanding of their impact on Ln acquisition (Rast 2008). 
It is of great scientific interest to study CLI in FE research for several reasons. First, in a situation of breaking into a new language, the learners’ previous linguistic experience comes from the languages they already know (Berthele, 2011). As opposed to en route learners, their learning is not affected by the knowledge they already acquired of the TL and there is a need to fill the knowledge gap in the TL (De Angelis, 2007). Hence, the use they make of their Lns can advocate for (or challenge) some of the already existing theories and claims about CLI (Bardel & Falk, 2007). Secondly, following Rast’s (2008) call for more investigation into learner’s prior knowledge influence when starting to learn a new L2, examining bi- or multilinguals processing at the very initial stages of acquisition can shed light on the interaction (or competition) between various languages in learners’ linguistic repertoire. 
The three pioneering FE studies concerned with linguistic transfer at FE were conducted by Singleton and Little (1984), Hendriks and Prodeau (1999) and Lalleman (1999). Singleton and Little (1984) exposed English native speakers without previous knowledge of German to a written text in Dutch (CVs of Dutch native speakers). The hypothesis they aimed at testing was that knowledge of another Germanic language close to Dutch will enhance comprehension in ab initio learners. Results showed that the knowledge of German contributed significantly to comprehension and to the feeling that the task was easier. In fact, participants with fair knowledge of German performed better than those without any knowledge of a Germanic language other than English in seventeen out of twenty questions. That authors concluded that “the comprehension of utterances in an unfamiliar language is facilitated if one can draw on knowledge of related languages, and that that the closer the relation between the languages one knows and the unfamiliar language, the greater will be one’s degree of understanding of utterances in the latter” (Singleton and Little, 1984, p. 16).
Similarly, Hendriks and Prodeau (1999) compared two French native speakers, one with advanced L2 German skills and another with advanced L2 English, on their learning of word order in Dutch sentences. Despite their lack of previous knowledge of Dutch, advanced L2 German speaker was able to correctly reproduce the verb second word order in Dutch sentences (present equally in German), which was not the case of an advanced L2 English speaker. Thus, the study evidenced transfer from a typologically similar L2. On the other hand, in order to test Full Transfer/Full Access Hypothesis (Schwartz & Sprouse, 1996) Lalleman (1999) exposed speakers of various L1s to Dutch (unknown to participants) for 3-6 weeks to examine their acquisition of a grammatical phenomenon (gapping). The grammaticality judgement test showed no significant differences between the speakers of different L1s, challenging the transfer hypothesis. 
In sum, these studies concerned with CLI at the very initial stages of learning showed first evidence of the influence of previous language knowledge, not exclusively the L1, on the processing of a novel FL. The 21st century brought about a considerable number of FE studies with a much broader scope of analysis. Several included previous linguistic knowledge among other possible factors affecting initial L2 learning. As some of the studies discussed in the following section have been described in detail in previous sections, the following summaries focus solely on the results concerning the role of previous linguistic knowledge influence on learning at FE. 
Park and Han (2008) investigated how English and Japanese native speakers process written input in Korean at FE. Given the typological similarities between Japanese and Korean, Japanese speakers were expected to be more successful at processing and comprehension than English native speakers. To measure processing, participants were asked to underline what they thought stood out in the passage and to write some questions about the passage. Contrary to VanPatten’s (1996) primacy of meaning principle, after the first reading both English and Japanese native speakers tended to focus on form-related features (such as frequency or saliency) and no major difference between the two groups was observed. The change occurred, however, at second exposure when the participants were equipped with some translation (six Korean words). While English native speakers questioned mainly the form-related text’s features, Japanese speakers asked questions related to meaning. Therefore, the authors suggest that initially participants processed the information equally (or, at least, focused on the same language features), but a minimal amount of translation favoured meaning processing based on L1 knowledge of a language related to the TL. 
Another study that showed previous linguistic knowledge’s impact on initial processing was conducted by Han and Liu (2013). When Japanese and English native speakers were exposed to AV Chinese input, they showed an overall low input processing (based on top-down strategies). However, as far as L1’s influence is concerned, Japanese speakers significantly outperformed English speakers on elicited imitation, which the researchers explain by a historical affinity in lexicon and phonology in Chinese and Japanese. 
Previous linguistic knowledge has also been considered as a factor playing role in initial processing at the VILLA project (Dimroth et al., 2013) which aimed at measuring a series of both input and learner-related factors at FE. In their study on Polish learning by French native speakers in classroom settings, Rast (2008) also analysed participants’ available knowledge at FE to the TL. French NSs (n = 34), participants who had not received any instruction in the TL, were divided into two groups to perform word translation task. Some participants had some knowledge of German, Dutch, Spanish, Italian and Russian as their L2. Overall, the average correct translation rate was 18%, although an analysis by participants’ linguistic repertoire revealed relevant patterns. The three words that achieved a translation rate of 90-100% (film, mama and informatykẹ), as well as the six words which achieved a 76-82% recognition rate, were highly transparent in participants’ L1, therefore, their prior linguistic knowledge may have aided the translation. A pattern concerning an L2 knowledge started to reveal as informants with some knowledge of German and Russian performed better than other participants (67%-58% recognition rate vs. 23% recognition rate for informants with knowledge of Romance L2s). In the case of words with the lowest recognition rate, informants with some knowledge of Russian outperformed other participants by achieving 18% recognition rate as opposed to 0-3% recognition rate of the rest of the group. These results led researchers to propose ‘a continuum of lexical transparency’ (Rast, 2008, p. 94) reigned by three principles:
1)	The closer to ‘identical’ the word in the informant’s known language is, the higher the chances that it will be recognised.
2)	The more known languages there are that contain formal equivalents with some resemblance to the target word, the higher the chance that it will be recognised.
3)	Points 1 and 2 are only true in cases where an equivalent form-meaning mapping occurs (the problem of false cognates). 
Therefore, these principles are especially relevant to keep in mind when conducting FE studies with languages that highly resemble each other, as it is in the case of Catalan/Spanish and Italian. 
Shoemaker and Rast (2013) exposed French native speakers to a 6.5-hour intensive Polish course in order to investigate the effect of words’ transparency (defined as the similarity to the L1) on input processing. Results revealed that not only were the participants able to segment transparent lexical items from speech without any previous exposure, but that after 6.5 hours of exposure highly transparent words were extracted and recognized significantly better than other words. These results were in line with Rast and Dommergues (2003) study, which also found a significant effect of transparency on input processing at FE. Shoemaker and Rast (2013) attribute the role of transparency to the fact that their participants were experienced language learners who knew English and a Romance language as their L2 and L3, so as they all had “previous experience with processing a novel acoustic stream, they no doubt had certain strategies already in place” (Shoemaker and Rast, 2013, p. 12), meaning that they relied on their previous linguistic knowledge. 
On the other hand, Sasson et al. (2025) focused on exploring the potential impact of L2 proficiency level on incidental and intentional vocabulary learning at FE. 72 university students, all learners of English, were divided into two groups according to their English proficiency level (high vs. low proficiency) and exposed to 80 Welsh words for the first time. Overall, although both groups learned from the input, the high-proficiency group outperformed the low-proficiency learners on both incidental and intentional learning. In consequence, authors suggest that multilingual experience benefits learning at FE through improving both necessary skills and cognitive abilities for foreign language learning.
While CLI research establishes that the learner uses the resources from their prior linguistic experience when approaching a novel FL, it primarily focuses on the bidirectional effects of existing linguistic systems. However, to fully explore learners’ cognitive resources proceeding from their linguistic repertoire at first encounter with a novel language, researchers propose the construct of the initial state. This concept addresses the fundamental nature of the individual linguistic architecture available to the learner at the very beginning of the acquisition process. Falk and Bardel (2024) challenged the notion of initial state (IS) as equal to initial acquisition stage, claiming that the IS of SLA depends on both learners’ L1 and the target language. In their study, 20 Swedish native speakers performed a Grammaticality Judgement Test (GJT) on twelve sentences in Norwegian, Dutch, and Finnish (36 sentences per participant, out of which 50% per language were correct, and 50% per language were incorrect). The results reflected the impact of typological similarities between the L1 and the TL on the GJT: Swedish NSs achieved 90% of correct answers in Swedish sentences, around 33% in Dutch, and only 7% in Finnish, in which the great majority of the answers (86.7%) were ‘I don’t know’. Moreover, researchers found that the success on GJT heavily depended on lexical similarities to the participants’ L1. Researchers conclude the following on the is: 
Considering previous research and our results, we are not convinced that researchers always denote the same thing with the term IS. Also, we argue that typological lexical similarities between background languages and target languages exert a huge impact on the duration of the IS. (Falk & Bardel, 2024, p. 10)
To conclude, the extensive body of FE research conducted over the past two decades identifies FL learners existing linguistic repertoire as a determinative factor in the initial processing of a novel language. While there is a broad consensus that CLI and transparency must be accounted for in FE research, the specific mechanisms and the extent to which typological proximity modulates lexical processing during these initial stages of acquisition remain an essential area for further empirical inquiry.
Nevertheless, learners’ abilities to break into a new language at initial stages are not only determined by their previous linguistic knowledge. Several cognitive variables have been explored in SLA to assess their role in language learning. Among them, language learning aptitude (LA) and working memory (WM) have been frequently investigated (Dimroth et al., 2013; Olender, 2012; Werkman, 2022; Miralpeix et al., 2023; Brown & Hulme, 1992; Papagno et al., 1991; Service & Craik, 1993; Dimroth et al., 2013; Han & Liu, 2013; McDonough & Trofimovich, 2016; Hofweber et al., 2022, 2023). Research suggests that there could be a positive correlation between these variables and successful learning at FE to AV input. In the following sections, a brief description of current knowledge and state of research on LA and WM is offered with further arguments in favour of incorporating the two variables in the study. 
2.3.3.2. Language Aptitude (LA) 
LA was first defined by Carroll (1981) as an individual’s “initial state of readiness and capacity for learning a FL and probable facility in doing so” (p. 86). In other words, the Carrollian concept of aptitude refers to ‘a special talent’ that makes some learners learn FLs more effectively than others. Further research in the context of SLA has led to relevant advances in the field and reshaped how LA is perceived in modern investigation. For instance, DeKeyser and Koeth (2011) advocate for the componential nature of LA construct and suggest defining it as “cognitive aptitudes” in plural instead of “language aptitude”, as each component of aptitude construct interacts with different aspects of L2 learning and processing. Simialrly, Wen et al. (2017) suggest, aptitude should be perceived as “an umbrella term consisting of a set of cognitive abilities” (p. 2) and it should not be confused with more general terms such as ability or intelligence. The conceptual difference between aptitude and ability is very subtle, as it refers primarily to the context in which these terms are used. In other words, aptitude can be perceived as a sort of specific ability that is stable and resistant to training, and which ensures a better achievement in language learning. 
As far as the distinction between aptitude and intelligence is concerned, although Sasaki (1996) showed no correlation between aptitude elements and general intelligence, Li (2016b) found a strong correlation (r = .64) between academic intelligence and LA. Sparks et al (2011) explain the strong correlation between LA and intelligence as the result of similar subtest and underline the fact that these correlations are not strong enough to prove that both constructs are identical.  Thus, even though there is an overlap, LA is more predictive of L2 proficiency than intelligence.
Research in LA can be divided into two main stages: predictive stage (from the 60s until late 2000) and theory construction phase (current research) (Wen et al., 2019). The first stage, also called macro-approach (Skehan, 2015a) stems from Carroll’s book Human Cognitive Abilities (1993), in which he distinguished between three levels of cognitive skills: general intelligence, general abilities with some specialisation, and domain-specific abilities, such as LA. According to this first stage of LA research, aptitude was a stable characteristic of each learner that was determinant of their learning success implicated especially in initial stages of learning. Therefore, this line of research focused on LA’s predictive power for L2 proficiency and ultimate attainment. 
Overall, extensive research affirmed that LA is a robust and stable predictor of SLA success across the lifespan. Studies by Harley and Hart (1997) and Abrahamsson and Hyltenstam (2008) establish LA as a significant determinant of achievement for both early and late starters, observing that specific aptitude components—such as memory versus analytical ability—shift according to the learner's age. This predictive power is fundamental: Sparks (2009) demonstrated that cognitive and affective factors account for approximately 66% of proficiency variance, with LA exerting a stronger influence than L1 skills. Similarly, Doughty (2019) utilized the MLAT and Hi-LAB batteries to confirm that LA consistently outperforms prior language learning experience as a predictor of proficiency.
This early LA research framework set by Carroll became a starting point for a series of studies on LA’s role in SLA and led to the creation of a new stage of research on aptitude—the theory construction phase—which emphasises the explanatory power of LA as an ID in the SLA context (Ellis, 2004; Robinson, 2005, 2012; Skehan, 2012, 2016; Wen & Skehan, 2016). Research in this stage tends to perceive LA in a multidisciplinary perspective, applied to cognitive science, educational psychology, neuroscience, and applied linguistics, and aims to consider the interaction of LA with multiple factors. For this reason, the theory construction stage is also called interactional (Li, 2019, p.84) or micro-approach research (Skehan, 2015). For instance, correlation between LA and variables such as motivation, anxiety, intelligence, or WM are explored. Additionally, this interactional research examines the role of LA depending on learning context or condition, for example, with corrective feedback, in implicit vs. explicit learning conditions, or with deductive vs. inductive instruction. For instance, in a meta-analytic review of this explanatory potential, Li (2016a) identified varying degrees of association between LA and other constructs, noting strong correlations with academic and working memory, a negative relationship with anxiety, and a low correlation with motivation. Empirical work by Dąbrowska (2018) further differentiated the influence of LA, nonverbal IQ, and exposure on proficiency, demonstrating that while LA shows no predictive power for vocabulary size in native speakers, it significantly accounts for 14% of the variance in L2 learners' vocabulary attainment. 
Beyond assessing direct correlations, research has focused on how LA interacts with specific instructional variables, examining its role in relation to corrective feedback (Linck et al., 2014), explicit versus implicit instruction (Carpenter, 2008; Robinson, 1997, 2002), and inductive versus deductive learning paradigms (Erlam, 2005; Hwu et al., 2014; Hwu & Sun, 2012). Additionally, research into LA in different learning context led to distinguishing between the two dimensions of the LA: the explicit language aptitude (ELA) and implicit language aptitude (ILA) (Granena, 2013a, 2013b). In this sense, ILA “can be defined as those cognitive abilities that facilitate implicit learning and processing of an L2 […] in the absence of (1) conscious intention to learn, (2) conscious awareness of the fact that we are learning, and (3) conscious attribution of any noticed change to the effects of learning” (Granena, 2020, p. 7). In Granena (2013a), principal components analysis (PCA) revealed two distinct aptitude dimensions within the LLAMA test (Meara, 2019) battery: while LLAMA B, E, and F were strongly interrelated and associated with analytical ability and general intelligence, LLAMA D (sound recognition) emerged as a separate component more closely linked to memory and sequence learning. Consequently, the study suggests that the LLAMA battery captures both explicit and implicit language aptitude, allowing for divergent performance profiles across its subtests. Moreover, Granena (2016) points out a correlation between explicit aptitude measures (LLAMA B, LLAMA E and LLAMA F) and rational-analytical profile (Pacini & Epstein, 1999), as well as between implicit aptitude measures (LLAMA D and SRT task) and experimental-intuitive profile (Pacini & Epstein, 1999). The authors suggest that such a finding advocates for the distinction between the two cognitive dimensions of linguistic aptitude: the implicit vs. the explicit aptitude. 
Furthermore, work by Granena (2013b, 2014; Granena & Long, 2013) highlights the enduring relevance of implicit and explicit learning dimensions, suggesting that while sequence learning ability contributes significantly to attainment in both children and adults, the capacity for implicit learning gradually declines with age, thereby increasing the reliance on explicit learning for adult lexical development.
The two dimensions of language learning aptitude are currently under review and validation (Li & DeKeyser 2021; Godfroid & Kim, 2021; Suzuki & DeKeyser 2017). For instance, Li and DeKeyser (2021) define implicit learning aptitude as a componential construct that is different to working memory or traditional language aptitude as measured by MLAT, which causes individual differences between the learners, as well as predicts learning rate and ultimate attainment. Additionally, it consists of both domain-general and domain–specific cognitive abilities. Thus, researchers suggest that “the advent of implicit aptitude has changed the terrain of aptitude research, prompting researchers to reconsider the findings and implications of previous research, reconceptualize the cognitive foundation of SLA, and collect evidence to validate the construct validity of implicit aptitude” (Li & DeKeyser. 2021, p. 494). 
While Granena first introduced LLAMA D as a measure of implicit learning aptitude, more recent research by Iizuka and DeKeyser (2023) indicates that this subtest may not reflect a single implicit construct, as it did not correlate with other implicit measures such as the SRT task. More specifically, following Granena’s (2013b, 2019) claim that LLAMA D can be used as implicit learning aptitude measure, the study compared the scores between three different instructions conditions memorization, “just listen” and with sound check, as well as with other implicit learning abilities tasks (probabilistic SRT task and Available Long-Term Memory task) and three explicit learning aptitude measures (paired associates task, digit span task, and Stroop task). Results revealed that there was no significant difference in LLAMA D scores between the three conditions, showing that the instructions did not have a significant impact on performance. In addition, there was no significant correlation between the two implicit learning abilities measures (SRT and ALTM) and LLAMA D, “therefore, no clear evidence of a unitary construct of implicit learning aptitude was found” (Iizuka & DeKeyser, 2023, p. 16).
More recently, Roehr-Brackin et al. (2023) confirming the theoretical distinction between explicit and implicit cognitive dimensions in a study on elderly participants. The results demonstrated a clear age-related decline in explicit aptitude (LLAMA B and F); conversely, implicit measures (LLAMA D) remained stable, indicating that implicit linguistic processing abilities are largely preserved in later life. Despite challenges with task completion related to motor processing speed in the SRT task, the researchers conclude that core cognitive capacities for language acquisition remain intact, though performance is further mediated by individual variables such as employment status, prior linguistic experience, and subjective cognitive self-assessment.
While traditional “macro-approaches” established LA as a stable predictor of long-term SLA success (Carroll, 1981; Abrahamsson & Hyltenstam, 2008), modern “interactional” research focuses on how specific cognitive components facilitate learning under varying conditions (Skehan, 2015; Wen et al., 2017). For the ab initio learner navigating multimodal input, the distinction between ELA and ILA becomes paramount. In this context, the LLAMA battery (Meara, 2019) has emerged as a primary tool for measuring these dimensions, though its internal validity, particularly regarding the implicit measures of LLAMA D, remains a subject of empirical debate (Bokander & Bylund, 2020; Iizuka & DeKeyser, 2023). 
LLAMA is a free, online short test based on the previous MLAT. It is language neutral and based on a northern Canadian indigenous language. The test is composed of four sub-tests (LLAMA_B, LLAMA_D, LLAMA_E and LLAMA_F), each one tapping a different LA component. LLAMA_B is designed to measure vocabulary learning (associative memory). Participants are presented with twenty pictures of invented objects and by moving the cursor on each picture, they see the name of each object (in the new language). Then, they have two minutes to learn the names. When the test starts, the participants are asked to click on the picture that corresponds to the name displayed on screen. 
LLAMA_D aims to measure implicit learning by assessing participants’ ability to recognise sounds they have previously heard. The test consists of a set of made-up words presented orally in one series, where after each word participants must decide whether the word they heard is new or repeated. 
LLAMA_E measures sound-symbol correspondence task by presenting participants with an alternative alphabet and subsequently testing their spelling abilities. Twenty combinations of one letter and one number corresponding to one syllable are displayed on screen for the participants to learn their pronunciation in two minutes (they can hear the pronunciation by clicking on the symbol). In the test, they hear a two-syllable word once and they must click on its spelling when twenty combinations of two symbols are displayed on screen. 
Finally, the LLAMA_F taps on grammar learning. In the learning part of the test, participants are given five minutes to explore an artificial [PO8.1]language grammar: they have a set of twenty buttons and by clicking on each, they can see a sentence in an artificial language and a picture that depicts this sentence. In the test part, a picture is displayed on screen and participants are asked to organise words to formulate a sentence that describes the picture. 
The validity of the LLAMA test battery has been explored by a series of empirical studies. Correlations were found between LLAMA tests (specific tests or a whole battery) and the following language components: speech fluency and grammatical and lexical complexity (Saito, 2017, 2019; Saito & Hanzawa, 2016), grammatical knowledge (Yalçin & Spada, 2012; Hamrick, 2015), and global proficiency (Artieda & Muñoz, 2016; Larson-Hall & Dewey, 2012). In a detailed analysis of the four test batteries, Rogers et al. (2017) proved that the test’s results were robust regardless of learners’ background variables, showing that the LLAMA test is a reliable measurement of aptitude in a wide range of population (although it needs some adjustments to be used with young learners). However, Bokander and Bylund (2020) examined the LLAMA tests internal validity with data collected from 350 participants. Based on classical item analysis, Rasch analysis and principal component analysis, they found that only LLAMA B fit a latent trial model with accuracy, suggesting that some LLAMA subtests (especially D and E) have low internal consistency and suffer from ceiling effects. Thus, researchers suggest that the battery needs some improvements to enhance its internal validity.
Other validated LA test batteries include the Modern Language Aptitude Test (MLAT) (Carroll & Sapon, 1959/2002), the High-Level Language Aptitude Battery (Hi-LAB) (Doughty et al., 2010) and the Cognitive Ability for Novelty in Language Acquisition-Foreign (CANAL-F) (Grigorenko et al., 2000). The MLAT focuses on explicit comprehension through four core components: phonetic coding, grammatical sensitivity, inductive learning, and associative memory. In contrast, the Hi-LAB was designed to measure aptitude in advanced learners and talented late starters, prioritizing constructs such as implicit learning, processing speed, and auditory acuity; notably, research using this battery suggests a distinction between implicit memory and learning, with the former serving as a predictor of speaking proficiency. Finally, the CANAL-F framework centres on a learner's capacity to handle novelty and ambiguity through five encoding and transfer processes, using immediate and delayed conditions to specifically account for the role of working memory in linguistic encoding.
Language aptitude serves as a critical determinant of vocabulary acquisition during FE to AV input, as it fundamentally modulates the cognitive efficiency with which learners perceive, encode, and retain novel lexical items in real-time. Despite a series of indices of a relevant role of LA in the initial stages of SLA (French & O’Brien, 2008; Dahlen & Caldwell-Harris, 2013; Morgan-Short et al., 2012; Ettlinger et al.2014; Hamrick, 2015; Li, 2016), until now, only three FE studies investigated a possible effect of LA on vocabulary acquisition from AV input. As previously mentioned, although some of the studies described in the following paragraphs have been explored in the first section of the literature review, in this section we will focus uniquely on their findings concerning the role of LA at initial learning stages. 
Miralpeix et al. (2023, 2024) examined a correlation between LLAMA_B test score and a 15-item meaning recognition test after 6 minutes of exposure AV in Polish (Polish subtitles with English audio) in native speakers of Spanish and Catalan. The results showed that LA was positively correlated to the vocabulary test and explained up to 24.3% of the test scores, assisting meaning recognition of the five most difficult TWs. Nevertheless, neither of the two replications of Miralpeix et al. (2023) reported a significant effect of LA on vocabulary test scores. Olender (2021) found no significant correlation between LA (LLAMA_B) and acquisition in Polish native speakers exposed to Spanish subtitles for the first time, and neither did Werkman (2022) after exposing Dutch native speakers to Romanian input (using LLAMA_B and LLAMA_D).
To justify this divergence in results, large differences in participants mean LLAMA test scores must be considered. In Miralpeix et al. (2023), participants achieved a mean score of 10.28 out of 20 in the LLAMA_B test, as compared to the score of 5.8 in Olender (2021). In the case of Werkman (2022), Dutch native speakers scored on average 4.3 on LLAMA_B and 8.13 on LLAMA_D test. Given such differences between the mean scores, the researchers concluded that LA only plays a role in initial processing when it is high enough. In other words, low linguistic aptitude (such as in the case of Olender, 2021, and Werkman, 2022) does not assist learning at FE, as opposed to a rather high aptitude (Miralpeix et al., 2023) which explains vocabulary acquisition at FE. 
In addition, research suggests that the role of LA in SLA depends on the difficulty of the task itself. For instance, Yalçin & Spada (2016) found that grammar inferencing component of LA, as measured by LLAMA F, contributed to acquisition of a difficult grammar structure (passive form), but it did not seem to have an impact on the acquisition of an easy structure (past progressive) in L2 English learners. This could also explain why Spanish L1 learners of Polish, a rather difficult language, relied on their LA in Miralpeix et al. (2023) more than Polish L1 speakers learning Spanish (Olender, 2021), which is morphologically and orthographically less complex than their L1. 
Collectively, these studies on the role of LA at FE to AV input posit a "difficulty threshold" that moderates the predictive power of aptitude. Findings suggest that LA emerges as a significant predictor only when this threshold is exceeded, a phenomenon dependent on either sufficiently high learner aptitude or high task complexity. This interaction is illustrated by typological distance, where the cognitive burden of acquisition changes according to the language pairing (e.g., Spanish speakers learning Polish versus Polish speakers learning Spanish).
In sum, the emergence of interest in LA research decades after its first definition by Carroll and colleagues led to relevant findings and allowed to establish some evidence on the nature and the role of aptitude, nowadays perceived as a complex construct. LA appears to be stable over lifespan (Harley & Hart, 1997; Roehr-Brackin et al., 2023) and predictive for ultimate attainment (Abrahamsson & Hyltenstam, 2008; Sparks, 2009; Granena, 2013b, Granena & Long, 2013; Granena, 2014; Doughty, 2019). Moreover, it seems to be helpful for L2 learners especially in the first stages of acquisition (Dabrowska 2018; French & O’Brien, 2008; Dahlen & Caldwell-Harris,2013; and many others), although only when the difficulty threshold is exceeded (Olender, 2021; Werkman, 2022; Miralpeix et al., 2023). Consequently, investigating the role of LA from the earliest stages of exposure is essential, not only to refine current models of FE learning but also to broaden the empirical foundation of SLA research. 
2.3.3.3. Working Memory (WM)
While linguistic aptitude represents the specialized “readiness” or talent for language learning, the success of the ab initio learner is equally dependent on the online processing power available to handle incoming stimuli. This cognitive 'workspace' is known as Working Memory (WM).
Research on WM originated in psychology and cognition research and has now been a point of interest for SLA research for about three decades. Given the heterogeneity of models and interpretations of the construct, various definitions of WM have been formulated. Miyake and Shah (1999) defined WM as “those mechanisms and processes that are involved in the control, regulation and active maintenance of task-relevant information in the service of complex cognition, including novel as well as familiar, skilled tasks” (p. 405). For Baddeley (2012) WM is “a temporary storage system under attentional control that underpins our capacity for complex thought” (p. 1) that refers to the ability of brief maintenance and operating limited information chunks while completing mentally demanding tasks. 
As far as the specific field of SLA is concerned, Wen and Skehan (2016) define WM as “those multiple cognitive mechanisms and functions implicated in the temporary maintenance, access and control of limited number of pieces of linguistic information to facilitate the acquisition, representation, processing and development of various domains and activities in learning a L2”  (p. 80). 
The processing of authentic AV input requires a complex cognitive effort, where learners simultaneously integrate rapid, spontaneous speech with visual imagery and dynamic on-screen text. This intrinsic complexity often exceeds the learner’s processing resources. Drawing upon Cognitive Load Theory (CLT), a critical disparity emerges: while learners with higher cognitive capacity may demonstrate the facility to simultaneously process auditory and visual streams, those with more constrained resources are often forced to prioritize a single information channel to the detriment of the other. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced during the initial stage, as learners lack the established L2 linguistic knowledge to rely on.  However, before discussing in detail the potential role of WM at FE to AV input, it is crucial to understand the foundations of the construct and the element it is made up of. 
Based on cognitive research in memory (James, 1890; Hebb, 1949; Atkinson & Shiffrin, 1968), as well as on research on long-term memory (LTM) and cognition in patients with impaired short-term memory (STM), Baddeley and Hitch (1974) first presented their three-component system, nowadays known as WM. The central component was first named supervisor attentional system (SAS), which currently operates under the name of central executive. The SAS was believed to be responsible for executive functions, such as attention allocation or controlling resources, as well as cognitive processes engaged in higher-level cognition. The two subsystems in the model were the phonological loop, responsible for temporary storage of sound-based chunks of information with the possibility of articulatory rehearsal process, and visuospatial sketchpad, responsible for processing visual and spatial information (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974, Atkins & Baddeley, 1998). In addition, as research suggested that the central executive was not responsible for storage (Baddeley & Logie, 1999), episodic buffer was integrated in the model, which Baddeley (2015) defines as a multidimensional storage system responsible of combining information from the visuospatial and verbal subsystems which links it with information stored in LTM. Therefore, the current WM model by Baddeley and colleagues is composed of four elements: the central element – central executive – controlling two ‘slave’ systems, phonological loop (verbal material storage) and visual sketchpad (visual material storage), and an episodic buffer which combines pieces of information from both subsystems. 
As far as evidence from research is concerned, central executive, also referred to as executive working memory (EWM) measures were shown to highly correlate with intelligence measures (Engle et al., 1999), with language comprehension (Daneman & Carpenter, 1980) and with learning in general (Vicari & Carlesimo, 2002). Similarly, phonological loop, currently referred to as phonological short-term memory (PSTM) or phonological working memory (PWM), appears to be a ‘very helpful but not essential tool in language acquisition’ (Baddeley, 2015, p. 24) as it combines language (sound) and all language related information (e.g., signed information, lip reading, etc.). It was shown to be correlated with FL acquisition in both children and adults in several studies (Baddeley et al.,1998; Atkins & Baddeley, 1998; Engel de Abreu & Gathercole, 2012; Nicolay & Poncelet, 2013) and Baddeley (1993) suggested that high intelligence is not sufficient to overcome SLA problems if phonological loop is limited. Finally, there is still not much research on the role of visuospatial sketchpad (Baddeley, 2015), which is why its role in language learning remains uncertain. 
The multiple-component WM model by Baddeley and colleagues is currently considered one of the most influential in cognitive science (Wen, 2016) and is used as a research framework in six cognitive sciences  (Miller, 2003). Thus, the system that allows individuals to process both aural and visual information to commit to short-term memory and manipulate chunks of information in mentally challenging tasks has quickly become of interest for research in SLA, mainly as an ID that could vary among participants and could have an impact on L2 processing and acquisition. In terms of AV input processing in real time, these components potentially play a crucial role in a wide range of cognitive processes: phonological loop is used for new word segmentation from speech, visual sketchpad is responsible for image processing, and the episodic buffer serves as a “glue” to link the spoken language to the captions and the image.  
Importantly, other WM models were formulated parallelly to the multicomponent model. The North American tradition represented by Engle and Cowan, focused on the research on executive elements in language processing, mainly through reading and comprehension. Cowan (1999, 2005) put forward the embedded-processes model which is not completely opposed to Baddeley’s the multiple component model, yet it emphasises the role of attention focus and on diversity of inputs. For instance, a written word may transmit orthographic, phonological, and semantic information at the same time. Thus, the embedded-processes model postulates that one stimulus activates different kinds of features in LTM (Cowan, 1988, 1999), and that attention is limited to 3-5 pieces of information, yet it plays a crucial role in the processing (Cowan, 2001). Similarly, the controlled attention model by Engle (Engle et al.,1999; Engle 2002) puts attention in the main focus of WM. In the case of this framework, WM is a domain-general system driven by attention focus and limited by efficiency of controlled attention and lack of inhibitory control with no clear hierarchy between the components. It is important to note that both models advocate for a continuous interaction between WM and LTM, while the multiple-component model distinguishes clearly between the two concepts. 
In sum, the two stages of research with their corresponding WM models have been of reference to a vast body of cognitive research in the last decades. Nevertheless, currently ten different WM models coexist. Despite such divergence of  frameworks, there are some common elements of all models that could be considered the milestones of WM, such as a) its limited capacity for holding information, b) its character of a primary memory system composed of both executive and non-executive operations, and c) the bi-directional interaction between WM and both STM and LTM (Wen, 2016). The executive functions of WM are grouped as maintenance, rehearsal, manipulation and inhibitory control among executive processes, and non-executive functions as  encompassing, encoding, chunking, consolidating, restructuring, transforming and retrieving (Dehn, 2008; Dehn et al., 2015). On the other hand, all the models present some common limitations, such as lack of clear distinction between WM, STM and LTM, as well as lack of an account of an actual work conducted by WM (Dehn, 2008; Dehn et al., 2015). Taking all the models in the account, Wen (2016) points out that even though their frameworks differ in scopes or focus, they are not substantially different from each other. 
Given the complexity of the construct and a wide range of models, various types of tasks have been proposed to assess WM. Overall, WM measures are divided into simple and complex assessment, both tapping at different WM components. Simple memory span tasks were mainly used in European line of research and are designed to tap mainly on PWM or limited STM preventing the information actively held from decaying (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974). The most common simple memory span tasks are word span, letter span, or digit span tasks, which ask participants to memorise and repeat a series of numbers or words in reversed order. 
Moreover, given the language specificity of the two simple span tasks, a non-word repetition task has also been elaborated (Juffs & Harrington, 2011) as a language neutral task (or, alternatively, tasks in subjects’ L1) preferred to represent a new foreign vocabulary (Gathercole et al., 1994). In other words, in order to measure purely WM, L2 skills should not be involved in the performance to avoid the confusion between WM and L2 proficiency effects.  
However, some researchers point out that while these simple tasks tap mainly on storage ability, WM is a complex construct combining both storage and processing (Baddeley, 2012; Trevisol et al., 2017; Kim, 2018). As a solution, they propose complex span tasks, meant to measure the executive functions of working memory (EWM), such as operation span task (Turner & Engle, 1989), the reading span task (Daneman & Carpenter, 1980), or the speaking span task (Daneman & Green, 1986; Fortkamp & Bergsleithner, 2007). For instance, the reading span task (Daneman and Carpenter, 1980) consists in reading a sentence for comprehension and grammaticality judgement and memorising the last word of the sentence in order to recall a series of words at the end of each trial. According to Wen (2016), it is the most seminal and widely adapted measure of EWM nowadays and the results seem to correlate with comprehension scores (Daneman & Merikle, 1996). In addition, it taps both at processing and storage at the same time, making it a dual-task designed to measure two different WM functions. The speaking span task (Daneman & Green, 1986) is very similar in nature to the reading span task; however, the words are retained while orally generating sentences, not while reading. 
Finally, the operation span task (Turner & Engle, 1989) was elaborated to measure WM as a domain-general, and not domain-specific language cognitive ability. For this reason, the task consists of completing a mathematical operation and memorising a word for later recall. Both Turner and Engle (1989) and Engle et al. (1992) showed a positive correlation between operation span, reading span and speaking span results, putting forward a question about whether WM abilities are domain-general or domain-specific. In other words, one of the main current issues of WM are the contrasting views on WM as a general capacity system independent on task content, or as a domain-specific system depending on the task content (i.e., language, maths, etc.). 
Overall, language-related measures, such as reading or speaking span, seem to be highly sensitive to language comprehension and production measures (i.e., Daneman & Merikle, 1996). On the other hand, Rosen and Engle (1998) indicate that language-unrelated measures (e.g., operation span) better represent the EWM capacity. Thus, language-related and language-unrelated measures reflect a “common mechanism of information storage and processing” (Rosen and Engle, 1998, p. 211), making the two types of measures complementary to each other. Therefore, Miyake and Shah (1999) suggest that acomprehensive WM theory should be able to account for its both domain-general and domain-specific effects on processing and storage. 
Having established the methodological frameworks used to quantify WM capacity, it is essential to translate these metrics into the domain of SLA. When the construct of WM first emerged, it was mainly applied in L1 acquisition research as a potential individual difference with an impact on children language acquisition. Baddeley and colleagues focused on exploring the role of PWM on different language components in children through the use of simple memory span tasks. For instance, Gathercole and Baddeley (1989) found that non-word repetition task predicted children’s L1 vocabulary size, Gathercole and Baddeley (1990) showed a correlation between the non-word repetition and inefficiency in new vocabulary learning and Gathercole et al (1992) found a correlation between PWM and vocabulary learning overall. Researchers concluded that ‘the evolutionary purpose of the phonological loop is to serve as a language learning device’ (Baddeley et al., 1998, p. 158) and that it “plays an instrumental role in the acquisition and development of vocabulary” (Wen, 2016, p. 47). In consequence, these findings on the prominent role of WM in L1 acquisition arose interest in exploring the impact of WM on SLA. As Skehan (2019) points out, independently on the approach towards the existence of critical period for language acquisition (i.e., unified approach by Macwhinney, 2005, or critical period approach by DeKeyser, 2000), language acquisition after critical period, thus, SLA, depends on general learning and cognitive abilities. Similarly, the Fundamental Difference Hypothesis (FDH) put forward by Bley-Vroman (1990) posits that L1 acquisition is fundamentally different to SLA, as SLA implies the use of general adult learning abilities. For this reason, WM has been incorporated into SLA research as a cognitive ability that could potentially have an impact on language acquisition after the critical period. In the following paragraph, three approaches to WM role in SLA by Skehan, Ellis and Wen are presented. 
In Skehan’s (2016) information processing perspective on SLA, it is implied that, as opposed to the mostly implicit and automatic nature of L1 learning, L2 learning must be governed by more explicit and controlled processes (Harrington, 1992; McLaughlin 1995). In addition, as L2 grammar and vocabulary in SLA are in constant competence with L1 structures, SLA may require more cognitive resources than L1 acquisition. Thus, one of the cognitive factors responsible for explicit information processing in SLA could potentially be WM (Skehan, 2014, 2015a; Wen 2012, 2015). Based on these tenets, Skehan (2016) elaborated the Macro-SLA aptitude model in which he divided the SLA process o into three main stages (language input, central processing and language output) to which he ascribed the cognitive processes taking place and their corresponding cognitive abilities. In this model, WM appears to play a role in six out of nine L2 cognitive processes, making it a central factor in SLA. 
Another perspective on SLA which considers the role of WM is Ellis’ connectionist, usage-based and construction-oriented perspective (Ellis, 1996, 2012, 2013, etc.), in which acquiring an L2 is viewed as a process of acquiring linguistic sequences or language chunks of different lengths governed by general cognitive mechanisms such as frequency, saliency, etc. (Ellis, 2002). In such an approach, successful SLA means acquiring enough chunks or sequences to be able to use during communication. The main role of PWM in this process is “consolidating, entrenching and automatizing activation of stable long-term mental representations of novel phonological material such as individual words, morphemes and lexical sequences” (Ellis, 2012, p. 62). 
Finally, the third framework including WM in SLA processes is Wen’s (2016) integrated framework for WM and SLA research, in which SLA is a “multi-faceted, complex, dynamic, emergent and adaptive process” (Wen, 2016, p. 81). The role of WM in such processes is described by the phonological/executive-hypothesis (P/E hypothesis), according to which both PWM and EWM are present in different processes and at different stages of SLA. While PWM underlies the acquisition and developmental areas of SLA (vocabulary and grammar), EWM underlies cognitive demanding L2 sub-skills, such as learning and processing. 
Independently on the perspective applied, it is crucial to mention that over 80 studies on the impact of WM on different SLA aspects have been conducted leading to a vast body of evidence on the role of WM in SLA (Wen, 2016). The impact of PWM has been shown in formulaic sequences and collocation learning (Skrzypek & Singleton, 2013, 2014), as well as in morphosyntax and grammar learning (e.g. Williams & Lovatt, 2005; Verhagen & Leseman, 2016). In addition, studies indicate the role of both EWM and PWM in reading and listening (e.g. Miyake & Friedman, 1998;), interaction and conversation (e.g. Mackey & Sachs, 2012), speech production, and written production.  
Given the extensive empirical support for its role in linguistic processing, WM is now recognized as a fundamental learner factor in contemporary SLA research.  Specifically, for the purposes of this study, it is of a special interest to explore in depth the relationship between WM components and L2 vocabulary acquisition. Given that a vast body of research has found a link between L1 vocabulary acquisition and PWM (Gathercole & Baddeley, 1989, 1990; Baddeley et al., 1998; Gathercole et al., 1992, 1997, 1999; Masoura & Gathercole, 2005), several studies have investigated the potential correlations between working memory and L2 vocabulary acquisition. 
Papagno et al., (1991) showed that PWM deficit affected L2 vocabulary acquisition, evidencing a link between WM and vocabulary learning (see also Baddeley et al., 1998). Service and Craik (1993) and Service and Kohonen (1995) found that children's PWM strongly predicts their L2 vocabulary learning (see also Cheung, 1996). Atkins and Baddeley (1998) also found a significant correlation between PWM and L2 vocabulary learning gains in adults. Masoura & Gathercole (1999) found that both L1 and L2 vocabulary knowledge were significantly correlated to PWM independently of other factors such as age, etc. Finally, Speciale et al (2004), as well as Williams and Lovatt (2003), suggested that PW and phonological sequence learning ability contributed significantly to vocabulary learning in university students. Therefore, research on WM's impact on L2 vocabulary acquisition consistently shows that PWM enhances learning and is predictive of vocabulary gains in both children and adults. In other words, it appears that WM is heavily involved in L2 vocabulary acquisition (Ellis & Sinclair, 1996). 
While this body of research underscores the contribution of WM to L2 vocabulary acquisition, the specific role of WM during the initial stages of learning may differ from results found in en route learners. FE scenarios, characterized by the absence of prior linguistic knowledge and the high cognitive demand of processing novel, authentic input in real-time, present unique challenges on the cognitive capacity that may differ from those experienced in later stages of acquisition. Consequently, investigating whether the facilitative effects of WM persist under these conditions is essential for understanding the cognitive characteristics of early-stage learning. The following section examines empirical evidence regarding the predictive utility of working memory specifically within these critical, earliest phases of FL exposure.
Although there is very little research on the role of WM at FE, studies conducted on participants with a beginner level of the language or no knowledge of the TWs tested provide us with evidence on WM’s impact on initial processing (Brown & Hulme, 1992; Papagno et al., 1991; Service & Craik, 1993; Baddeley et al., 1998). Based on a wide body of evidence, Masoura and Gathercole (2005) claim that “in the initial stages of learning vocabulary in a new language, phonological memory plays a crucial role in supporting the construction of stable long-term memory representations” (p. 427). Similarly, Wright (2015) states that WM is associated with explicit or declarative knowledge in the pre-automatic stage, when the learners are ‘noticing, monitoring and using other explicit, consciously attended processes to control their language interpretation and production’ (p. 286). 
As far as FE studies are concerned, only few studies so far took WM into account in initial learning. For instance, McDonough and Trofimovich (2016) found no effect of WM on novel morphological pattern processing of Esperanto, and Hofweber et al. (2022, 2023), found no correlations between WM measures and accuracy at recognizing TWs among distractors after exposure to weather forecast in Swedish Sign language. Importantly, Han and Liu (2013) related WM to processing at FE using the reading span task. In their study, native English and Japanese speakers with no knowledge of Chinese (TL) were exposed to AV input Chinese and performed a reading span task to measure their WM. Results showed that WM was significantly correlated only to elicited imitation with focus on tone in both groups. Nevertheless, authors also state that ‘the Pearson correlation analyses turned up a number of intriguing, if not random, results’ which ‘raised a red flag about the test used in the study’ (Han & Liu, 2013: p. 161).  In fact, the reading span task was provided in English for both English and Japanese native speakers, thus, English native speakers have indeed outperformed Japanese speakers in WM scores. Such results raise questions about the reliability of the measurement, given that performing the reading span task in a non-native language seems to affect the results (Service et al., 2002). In consequence, the authors conclude that “the disparate results do not lend themselves to a coherent – let alone reliable – understanding of whether working memory played a role in the input processing by the ab initio learners” (Han & Liu, 2013: p. 162). Therefore, researchers should avoid employing language-based WM tests (e.g., reading span) in the L2 with ab initio populations, as insufficient target language knowledge impedes the collection of reliable performance data.
In conclusion, WM appears to be a crucial ID with a potentially significant impact on many aspects of SLA, with its important role in L2 vocabulary gains, attainment, and prediction. Although researchers argue for its impact on processing from the earliest stages of SLA (Masoura & Gathercole, 2005; Wright, 2015), research on the role of WM components at FE to a new language remains limited. Moreover, as Kormos (2013), Yalçin et al (2016) and Skehan (2016) point out, WM might have a significant impact on language processing at the very first contact with new input, as it is responsible for both short-term storage and manipulation of information.  The scarcity of studies exploring the potential role of WM in the process of breaking into the new language, as well as the potential of WM to moderate processing of input involving handling of sound, image, and written text simultaneously call for investigation into the role of WM learning an L2 at FE from AV input.  
Given almost parallel research on LA and WM as individual cognitive differences in SLA acquisition, a debate arose about the distinction between the two terms. More specifically, researchers started comparing the two constructs and considering the possibility of merging them as two complementary systems. For instance, a bias towards a prevailing role of WM as LA components can be seen in Hi-LAB testing (Doughty et al., 2010; Linck et al., 2013) and CANAL-F measurements (Grigorenko et al., 2000). Similarly, Sawyer and Ranta (2001) argue that “working memory capacity may be the key to elaborating the concept of language aptitude itself and to clarifying its relationship with the SLA process” (p. 340) and Miyake and Friedman (1998) put forward the “working memory as language aptitude hypothesis”, which puts WM in the central place of LA concept. Wen et al. (2017) indicate that the most recent trends in aptitude research tend to show a bias towards WM inclusion, which is why “working memory perspective on FL aptitude presents one promising avenue for advance” (p. 1).
In fact, Skehan’s (2016) Macro-SLA aptitude model discussed previously merges the two concepts by including WM in aptitude components. In the model, WM plays a crucial role in six out of nine L2 cognitive processes, being responsible for phonemic coding ability and language analysis ability. Similarly, Robinson (2005, 2007, 2013) put forward a view of LA as four broad aptitude complexes of lower-level cognitive abilities: noticing the gap, memory for contingent speech, meta-linguistic rule rehearsal, and memory for contingent text. This model also includes WM and PSTM as elements of LA. 
On the other hand, current empirical inquiry into the relationship between WM and LA consistently positions these variables as moderately related yet distinct constructs, indicating that WM cannot effectively replace LA as a singular measure of acquisition potential. Yalçin et al. (2016) demonstrated that while general LA and WM scores are correlated, they function as separate dimensions; notably, the correlation between WM and LLAMA_F (grammar) suggests that WM is essential in the cognitive manipulation required for rule inference. This relationship is further supported by Kim (2018) and Hackl (2018), who both observed significant correlations between WM and MLAT aptitude scores. However, the distinction between these constructs remains critical, as Kim (2018) found no significant correlation between WM and LLAMA_B (associative learning), reinforcing the conclusion that while WM contributes to cognitive processing in language learning, it operates as a unique construct that functions alongside, rather than instead of, language aptitude. 
Given the variety of findings regarding the role of LA and WM in language learning and their possible interaction at different stages of language processing, it is of great interest in this study to test these two factors in the very specific conditions of FE to AV input. Firstly, none of the previous FE studies on AV input explored WM as potential ID moderating processing and learning, and the findings for LA in the same condition were divergent (Miralpeix et al., 2023; Olender, 2021; Werkman, 2022). 
While the preceding discussion has established the foundational role of input and learner factors in shaping an individual's potential for vocabulary acquisition at FE, these variables provide only a partial picture of the learning process. To fully comprehend how these input and cognitive constraints and facilitators affect learning, it is necessary to examine the actual deployment of attentional and strategic resources during input processing. Consequently, this section shifts its focus from input and learner features observable processing behaviours. By integrating both offline processing measures¬—learners’ strategic behaviour—and online processing measures—eye-tracking—we can explore how learners actively navigate the multimodal demands of AV input and bridge the gap between cognitive factors and real-time interaction with input. 
2.4. Processing the Input: Vocabulary Learning Strategies and Eye-Tracking
Research on input processing is still scarce in comparison to research on acquisition at FE. While evidence of FL acquisition has been provided in classroom, laboratory and AV context, few studies have explored processing at the initial stage of L2 learning. Traditionally, research in SLA distinguishes between offline and online processing measures. Offline measures assess cognitive processes retrospectively, typically through post-exposure assessments such as performance tests or self-report questionnaires. Conversely, online measures capture processing dynamics in real-time; for instance, eye-tracking technology enables the investigation of attentional allocation as it unfolds. To provide a comprehensive exploration of AV input processing at FE¬—a domain that remains notably under-investigated—this study adopts a dual-methodological approach. Vocabulary Learning Strategies (VLS) are analysed as an offline metric, while eye-tracking data serves as the online processing measure.
2.4.1. Vocabulary Learning Strategies (VLS)
When considering the impact of learner factors in L2 processing, we need to take into account that learners vary not only in their language background and their cognitive characteristics (such as WM or LA), but also in their individual approach to language learning. For instance, students often differ in their reliance on their memory, their analytical abilities or different types of practice. In fact, some researchers argue that the use of strategies sets the difference between L1 and L2 learning (Pavicic-Takac, 2008), as an intentional strategic behaviour is typical of FL acquisition. Moreover, research has shown a correlation between the use of strategies and overall language learning success (Ahmed, 1989; Gu, 1994; Green & Oxford, 1995; Dreyer & Oxford, 1996; Gu & Johnson, 1996; Lawson & Hogben, 1996; Park, 1997; Schmitt, 1997; Kojic-Sabo & Lightbown, 1999; Fan, 2003, Griffiths, 2003, 2008; Kyungsim & Leavell, 2006) and that learners tend to use learning strategies for vocabulary more frequently than for other language skills (Pavicic-Takac, 2008). However, these studies have focused exclusively on experienced en route learners who had already developed and refined a repertoire of language learning strategies while learning a FL. 
Despite the fundamental role of language learning strategies (LLS) in 'cracking the code' of a new language, their investigation within FE paradigms remains scarce, with only a handful of studies (e.g., Dimroth et al., 2013; Singleton & Little, 1984; Miralpeix et al., 2024) incorporating strategic behaviour into their analysis. Moreover, in the context of intentional learning of this study, participants are likely to engage in active ‘code breaking’, makings this variable critical for exploring their input processing. Consequently, it is essential to explore the specific LLS employed during FE to a novel language to fill this gap and contribute both to initial stages and to language learning strategies research.
Research on learners’ strategic behaviour has a rather long tradition, resulting in different definitions of LLS. In fact, the first definitions stem from the behaviourist approach in the 1970s, in which LLS are defined as “the techniques or devices which a learner may use to acquire knowledge” (Rubin, 1975, p. 43). However, one of the most well-known definitions of strategies is the one by Oxford (1990) who defines them as “specific actions taken by the learner to make learning easier, faster, more enjoyable, more self-directed, more effective, and more transferable to new situations” (p. 8). More recently, Griffiths (2008, 2013) defined them as “activities consciously chosen by learners for the purpose of regulating their own language learning” (p. 36). Therefore, strategies come from an individual learner's decision and are a conscious element of language learning that they select and adjust according to their needs or their previous experience. 
In response to the criticism concerning LLS psychological and research validity (Seliger, 1983; Ellis, 1986; Dörnyei & Skehan, 2003; Dörnyei, 2005), Oxford (2017) conducted a content-analytical study that compiled thirty-three LLS definitions (twenty-seven of them strictly related to SLA field) and identified common elements and themes to propose an encompassing definition. The analysis resulted in the following LLS definition:
L2 learning strategies are complex, dynamic thoughts and actions, selected and used by learners with some degree of consciousness in specific contexts in order to regulate multiple aspects of themselves (such as cognitive, emotional, and social) for the purpose of (a) accomplishing language tasks; (b) improving language performance or use; and/or (c) enhancing long-term proficiency. (Oxford, 2017, p. 48)
Due to the complexity and diversity of conceptualizations of the construct, the theoretical framework of LLS encompasses a wide array of variables, including learning context, task specificity, and teachability. Among these, the establishment of a robust taxonomy is crucial for ensuring empirical consistency within the field.
The earliest classification of learning strategies implied a division in two (direct and indirect strategies) (Rubin, 1981) or three categories (cognitive, metacognitive, and social strategies) (O’Malley et al., 1985). Over the years of research, several different categorisation models were proposed (e.g. Oxford, 1990; Pintrich & Garcia, 1991; Purpura, 1999; Yang, 1999; Schmidt & Watanabe, 2001; Cohen et al., 2003; Oxford, 2011). Oxford (1990) elaborated her Strategy Inventory for Language Learning (SILL) dividing strategies into six categories (memory, cognitive, compensation, metacognitive, affective, and social) and then reduced it to four (cognitive, affective, sociocultural-interactive, and metastrategies) for greater cohesion (Oxford, 2011). Yang (1999) also developed a six-element categorization model, however, with the categories slightly differing from the ones applied by Oxford (1990): functional practice, cognitive-memory, metacognitive, formal-oral, social and compensation. Pintrich and Garcia (1991) as well as Purpura (1999) opted for a three elements model including comprehension, storage, and use. In 2001, Schmidt and Watanabe put forward a four categories model classifying strategies into cognitive, social, study and coping. Finally, Cohen et al. (2003) developed their Language Strategy Use Inventory according to the four language skills.  Thus, while commonalities exist in how strategies are grouped, the absence of a unified framework has drawn criticism from various scholars (Oxford & Cohen, 1992). Specifically, Cohen and Macaro (2007) question the utility of rigid classifications, asserting that strategies are “flexible and permeable” by nature and that taxonomies often fail to reflect their dynamic application in practice (p. 141).
However, learning strategies focused on vocabulary acquisition has received a special research attention. Oxford (2017) defines vocabulary learning strategies (VLS) as “teachable, dynamic thoughts and behaviours that learners consciously select and employ in specific contexts to improve their self-regulated, autonomous L2 vocabulary development for effective task performance and long-term proficiency” (p. 244). Up to now, few studies attempted to propose a VLS categorization. Ahmed (1989) identified 38 VLS and grouped them into five macro-strategies of memorization, practice, dictionary use, note-taking, and group work.  Brown and Payne (1994) studied vocabulary learning strategies as related to the steps students usually take when learning new vocabulary (having sources for discovering new words, getting a clear image of the forms of the new words, learning the meaning of the words, making a memory connection between the forms and the meanings of the words, and using the words).  Finally, Gu and Johnson (1996) proposed a comprehensive taxonomy comprising three distinct categories: metacognitive regulation (e.g., selective attention and self-initiation), cognitive strategies (encompassing guessing, dictionary use, note-taking, and memory encoding or rehearsal), and activation strategies (focused on the active deployment of newly acquired vocabulary in productive contexts).
A prominent framework in VLS research is the taxonomy developed by Schmitt (1997, 2000), which synthesized Oxford’s (1990) four-component model (social, memory, cognitive, and metacognitive) with the addition of determination strategies. Furthermore, Schmitt introduced a functional distinction between discovery strategies—focused on initial form and meaning decoding—and consolidation strategies, aimed at long-term retention. Consequently, the taxonomy comprises 58 items: nine determination (all discovery); eight social (split between five discovery and three consolidation); and three purely consolidation-based groups: memory (27), cognitive (9), and metacognitive (5) strategies. 
To empirically validate this framework, Schmitt (1997) conducted a large-scale survey-based study exploring VLS usage pattern, their perceived utility, and the developmental shifts in VLS use. Results from 600 Japanese EFL students revealed that the most frequently used strategies were using bilingual dictionaries (85%), guessing the meaning from context (74%) and verbal or written repetition (76%). Participants rarely relied on L1 cognates (11%), a finding that Schmitt justified by the study context and the distance between the L1 (Japanese) and the L2. As far as perceived helpfulness of VLS is concerned, once again the contestants prioritized using bilingual dictionaries (95%), but also asking teachers for paraphrasing (86%), analysing pictures and/or gestures (84%), searching for synonyms/antonyms (88%), etc. Furthermore, a longitudinal comparison across groups revealed a developmental shift. Younger learners favoured ‘shallow’ or mechanical strategies, such as memorising, flash cards, repetition. In contrast, mature learners preferred the use of strategies that allow deeper processing, for instance, creating associations, analysing linguistic elements, or imaging. Ultimately, Schmitt’s work provided an empirical foundation that has sustained the taxonomy’s prominence in VLS research for decades. 
More recently, Zhang (2011) introduced a classification theory for vocabulary learning strategies derived from confirmatory and exploratory factor analyses, which identified six distinct categories: four related to cognitive strategies and two related to metacognitive and affective factors. The study involved 296 Chinese university students who completed a 60-item vocabulary learning strategies questionnaire using a five-point Likert scale. A three-stage analysis was conducted. Confirmatory (CFA) and exploratory factor analyses (EFA) were employed to identify the categories accounting for the variance in the remaining 58 items. The CFA was used to evaluate whether four existing theoretical models could be applied to the data: Oxford’s (1990) six-factor model (metacognitive, cognitive, memory, compensation, affective, and social), Oxford’s (2011) four-factor model (metacognitive, cognitive, social, and affective), O’Malley and Chamot’s (1990) three-factor model (metacognitive, cognitive, and social/affective), and Gu and Johnson’s (1996) two-factor model (cognitive and metacognitive). Finally, the EFA was utilized to explore the structural dimensions of vocabulary learning strategies within the gathered data. Results led to identify three vocabulary learning strategies categories:
A)	Metacognitive strategies: strategies that are used to plan, organise and monitor the progress of learning;
B)	Cognitive strategies, comprising first encounter, building links between the new words and the previous lexical knowledge, guessing and word use;
C)	Affective strategies: students’ attitude towards vocabulary learning. 
Additionally, Zhang (2011) connected these four cognitive strategies to the five essential steps in vocabulary learning and explored the internal correlations between the three identified categories (cognitive, metacognitive, and affective). It was found that the metacognitive strategies are correlated to all the factors in the model, while the affective factors do not correlate with any other factors. The researchers conclude that affective factors play an important role in the general vocabulary learning process more than in particular steps of learning. In this way, Zhang (2011) proposes a three categories vocabulary learning strategies model that refers to previous models by re-using some of the categories (i.e. metacognitive, affective, etc.) but classifying them according to their mutual correlation and the role they play in the variance in responses.
The deployment of VLS is dynamic and mediated by diverse learner-internal factors ranging from age (Griffiths, 2003; Peacock & Ho, 2003; Victori & Tragant, 2003), gender (Wharton, 2000; Nisbet et al., 2005) and motivation (Oxford & Nyikos, 1989; Mochizuki, 1999), to proficiency (Dreyer & Oxford, 1996; Park, 1997; Nisbet et al., 2005) and  personality (Ehrman & Oxford, 1989, 1990, 1995; Wakamoto, 2000). Furthermore, VLS are linked to learners’ LA: Poschner (2018) found that while usage patterns are largely consistent across groups, high-aptitude learners show a significantly stronger preference for mnemonic strategies and synonyms. Consequently, research suggests that explicit instruction in VLS is necessary to enable all learners—particularly those of average aptitude—to apply strategies effectively (Macedonia et al., 2011; Wieland, 1990).
In conclusion, the evolution of VLS research reflects a shift from descriptive taxonomies (Ahmed, 1989; Brown & Payne, 1994) towards empirically validated frameworks (Schmitt, 1997; Zhang, 2011; Oxford, 2017). Although these models differ in their specific categorizations, they share a fundamental distinction between strategies used to decode form and meaning upon initial encounter and strategies employed to stabilize and retain the new lexical item over time. Schmitt’s (1997) finding that learners gradually move from mechanical repetition to deeper, associative processing provides a critical longitudinal perspective, while Zhang’s (2011) model highlights the central role of metacognition in the learning process. 
However, it is important to note that VLS foundational taxonomies were largely developed within the context of traditional classroom environments or reading-based tasks. At the FE stage, particularly when learners are presented with the authentic high-speed stimuli of AV input, the strategic repertoire of the learner must adapt to a much higher cognitive load. In such environments, the reliance on external tools like dictionaries are necessarily replaced by real-time, online processing strategies. The following section, therefore, examines how these strategic behaviours manifest when the learner must "crack the code" of a new language through the coordination of sound, image, and on-screen text.
2.4.1.1. VLS in AV Environments 
Although empirical research on VLS within AV environments remains limited, existing research hypothesizes that AV input needs a complex strategic repertoire. Rubin (1995), for instance, suggests that learners simultaneously employ cognitive strategies, such as predicting content via visual cues or identifying cognates, and metacognitive strategies, including goal-setting and selective listening (Thompson & Rubin, 1996). Regarding specific applications within AV input, several items from Oxford’s (2017) comprehensive list appear particularly relevant, for instance, intentional association, or contextual guessing. Similarly, within the domain of discovery strategies, Schmitt’s (1997) taxonomy highlights determination tools such as discerning parts of speech, analysing root/affix structures, and identifying cognates, all of which are potentially vital for novice learners at the FE stage.
Furthermore, certain consolidation strategies traditionally associated with later stages of learning may actually emerge at FE: for example, memorization, the analysis of orthographical or phonological forms, and grouping may play a role as learners attempt to process new lexical forms perceived through AV input. Beyond purely cognitive factors, some researchers argue that the low-anxiety environment inherent in AV contexts, combined with learner familiarity with AV media, may favour the deployment of affective strategies (Krashen, 1985; Borras & Lafayette, 1994). Finally, AV input provides an ideal environment for what Nyikos and Fan (2007) describe as contextualized vocabulary inference strategies. As a result of distributed recognition, repeated exposure (Nation, 1990), and the processing of rich semantic fields and collocations (Oxford & Scarcella, 1994; Hulstijn et al., 1996) facilitated by the nature of AV input, vocabulary inference strategies foster the transition from incidental exposure to intentional learning (Krashen, 1989). However, further empirical evidence is needed to accurately describe the real-time deployment of these strategies during FE.
In practice, studies exploring strategies use during exposure to AV input are rather scarce. One of the first studies to examine LLS in relation to AV input was conducted by Vanderplank (1988). After a nine-week long period of exposure to AV materials (interlingual subtitles condition), participants reported that they developed some learning and comprehension strategies, such as switching between the sound and the captions or learning to process the three information channels (video, sound, and captions) simultaneously. In his follow-up study, Vanderplank (1990) conducted a similar experiment over a three months period to find that, “there was evidence […] that paying close attention to the language used in programmes through note-taking or other aids to retention helped subjects produce an altogether higher level of English in terms of accuracy and specificity of language” (p. 226), as opposed to the learners who did not apply any strategies and chose to ‘sit back and enjoy the programmes’. 
Such findings align with Mayer’s active processing assumptions within the cognitive theory of multimedia learning (2014), which posits that “within each channel, and subject to capacity limitations, active learners select, organize, and integrate aspects of incoming words and pictures” (p. 103). In this framework, strategy use serves as the behavioural manifestation of the learner’s effort to mediate these incoming channels.  Therefore, processing of AV is active and intentional, although constrained by cognitive and capacity limitations. More recently, studies with experienced L2 English learners have reported evidence of both cognitive and memory strategies use with AV input (Lin, 2009, 2011; Mu, 2010), reflecting patterns similar to those observed in reading research. 
Furthermore, research suggests that substantial differences exist between strategies used in AV versus unimodal (e.g., text-only) input contexts. For instance, Lee and List (2019) compared the strategic processing of materials via text and video input. They found that participants exposed to AV materials reported significantly more selection strategies aimed at general comprehension, whereas participants in the text-only condition used higher-level strategies focused specifically on vocabulary. Such results potentially reflect the cognitive load involved in multi-channel AV input, where learners are compelled to deploy their strategic resources in real time processing, as opposed to reading characterized by learner-paced analysis and regression.  
Overall, despite the limited number of studies analysing the use of VLS with AV input, current research indicates that learners can actively mediate multi-channel AV information through strategic processing. This shift toward specialized processing has recently led to the development of more precise measurement instruments, such as the VLS-AV questionnaire validated by Teng (2025), which accounts for the unique strategic requirements of learning from AV input. These modern frameworks suggest that while the cognitive load is higher in AV input, the strategic repertoire of the learner allows for successful "code-breaking" even at FE. 
2.4.1.2. VLS at FE 
To date, research investigating VLS at FE stage has been predominantly limited to written modalities. Studies examining how ab initio learners navigate novel languages have traditionally used reading-based tasks (e.g., Singleton & Little, 1984; Han & Sun, 2014; Park, 2014), leaving a significant empirical gap regarding dynamic, multimodal environments. In fact, Miralpeix et al. (2024) represents the only known investigation to explore the strategic repertoire of learners encountering a novel language through AV input.
Nyikos and Fan (2007) suggest that ab initio learners would tend to use less complex strategies than proficient learners and would mainly rely on repetition strategies (Cohen & Aphek, 1981; Tinkham, 1993; Gu & Johnson, 1996; Lawson & Hogben, 1996; Schmitt, 1997). Potentially, learners at FE could also benefit from associative strategies, such as associating sounds or meaning to similar L1 words or to other L2 words (Cohen & Aphek, 1981). Therefore, researchers hypothesize that FE learners tend to show preference towards top-down strategies. This methodology implies a macro-to-micro approach, moving from holistic understanding towards specific detail. On the contrary, bottom-up strategies entail data-driven approach, where the learner starts the decoding process with basic, individual units to eventually decode meaning through the accumulation of these components.
Early investigations into FE processing underscore a heavy reliance on cross-linguistic similarities and structural cues. Singleton and Little (1984) analysed the specific strategic repertoire used to decode Dutch at FE. The study revealed that learners, regardless of their linguistic background, primarily engaged in cross-linguistic lexical association, seeking formal similarities between the novel Dutch words and known languages to map form to meaning. Furthermore, participants used contextual inferencing, leveraging fragments of understood text to interpret unfamiliar surrounding portions. There was also a notable reliance on input cues, where learners benefitted from the spoken version of the text to decode the written input. Ultimately, these findings suggest that FE strategic processing is characterized by a simultaneous reliance on internal linguistic strategies and the specific features of the multimodal input.
Similarly, Han and Sun (2014) used a questionnaire with three open question to explore strategies of FE learners of Norwegian presented with a short text in the novel TL. Results revealed that learners applied very similar strategies for both free-recall and gap filling exercise. In the free recall, these novice learners relied mainly on their prior language knowledge, familiar or repeated items, or memorizing words. However, the authors underline the fact that such behaviour reflects top-down processing, as the participants seemed to process the input using strategies that were not specific to the TL. Regarding the gap-filling exercise—designed to assess form-meaning mappings—participants reported a heavy reliance on memory and guessing. This suggests that at the earliest stages of acquisition, learners frequently resort to top-down strategies to navigate novel input.
In another study investigating ab initio learners processing, Park (2014) analysed learners strategies when processing written input in an unknown language. The study focused on native speakers of English (n = 30) and Japanese (n = 30) exposed for the first time to a written text in Korean, in which they had to visually mark elements they were focusing on, and then they were allowed to ask questions about the text. 29 English speakers also decided to verbalise their thoughts while processing in a think-aloud exercise.  The results revealed that as much as 70% of participants focused on repetition, and 63.3% noticed punctuation and commas. Around one third of the participants noticed other structural aspects, such as short sentences, the title, spaces between characters, or the direction of reading. 26.6% also suggested that it was similar to Hebrew. Finally, around 10% of participants mentioned looking for cues on capital letters and indentation for new thoughts. Authors conclude that, apparently, ab initio learners’ processing relied mostly on previous linguistic knowledge and structural cues, evidencing a strong preference of novice learners for top-down strategies. 
More recently, Miralpeix et al. (2024) explored the use of strategies in a FE study with AV input. 153 Catalan/ Spanish native speakers were exposed to a 3-minute advert in English with Polish subtitles twice. They were explicitly asked to learn as much as possible of the new language in the video. In post-viewing questionnaire, participants had to answer one open-ended question about which strategies they used to learn new words. Results showed that participants relied mostly on cognitive strategies, such as reading subtitles, using imagery and visual memory, focusing on repeated words, and comparing the words with previously known languages. An innovation of this study is the systematic alignment of strategies with the steps of vocabulary acquisition established by Brown and Payne (1994). Specifically, they demonstrated how learners operationalize these steps within AV context: subtitle reading and cross-linguistic comparison facilitate 'drawing a clear image of word forms'; the use of imagery and further cross-linguistic mapping enable 'learning the meaning'; and repeated viewing, combined with visual memory, serves to 'establish a memory connection between form and meaning.' By mapping these real-time behaviours onto a theoretical framework, the authors provide a novel understanding of how ab initio learners navigate the early stages of acquisition during video viewing. 
In summary, while the LLS literature has occasionally been critiqued for its methodological heterogeneity, it offers a vital framework for understanding the "online" mental processes of the ab initio learner. The current state of the art advocates for a deeper examination of how these strategies are deployed within AV environments, where the learner must manage a high cognitive load in real-time. As Nyikos and Fan (2007) suggest, the contextual richness of AV provides a unique opportunity for strategic "code-breaking".
Although the LLS research provides fundamental insights into the conscious and retrospective choices of learners, they remain offline measures subject to the limitations of human memory and self-perception. To achieve a more objective understanding of how these strategies are deployed in real-time, it is necessary to move beyond self-reporting and examine the online allocation of attention. Consequently, the following section explores the potential of using eye-tracking technology as a primary methodology for capturing the moment-by-moment cognitive online processing that occurs during exposure to AV input.
2.4.2. Online Processing via Eye-Tracking
Amidst the growing emphasis on process-oriented research in SLA, eye tracking has emerged as a robust method for quantifying cognitive effort. This technology provides an online measure of processing and of cognitive effort put into processing from the very first to the last second of contact with the input (Conklin et al., 2018). While this methodology has been extensively validated in studies of en route learners in AV environments, it has been increasingly to investigate the initial stages of acquisition. Employing eye-tracking in FE studies provides a unique window into the cognitive architecture at first encounter with a novel language, enabling researchers to observe how learners process novel linguistic information before any proficiency has been established.
Therefore, our knowledge of processing AV input at FE remains limited. In fact, FE studies utilizing eye-tracking technology conducted up to date focused mainly on establishing whether ab initio learners read on-screen text in the novel language. For instance, after establishing that NSs read subtitles in their L1 in intralingual subtitling condition (L1 in both audio and text) (D’Ydewalle et al., 1987; D’Ydewalle et al., 1991),  therefore, reading subtitles is a rather mechanical behaviour that does not depend on the need or on the familiarity with subtitling, D’Ydewalle and De Bruycker (2007) took a step further and used eye-tracking to analyse reading behaviour in novice learners. Twelve adults (19-26 years old) and eight children (10-12 years old) native speakers of Dutch watched a 15-minute excerpt of a Swedish cartoon. The cartoon was presented in two conditions: standard (L2 audio and L1 subtitles) and reversed subtitling (L1 audio and L2 subtitles). The study contributed to the research initial processing by showing no significant differences between the time spent on reading in the two conditions, showing once again that reading subtitles is a rather natural habit and it does not depend on the actual utility of the text for achieving viewing comprehension. In other words, these findings prove that participants read subtitles automatically, even when they have no knowledge of the subtitling language. Consequently, these findings provide a foundation for future research regarding how ab initio learners navigate on-screen text and how these processing behaviours influence acquisition.
The first FE study which made use of eye tracking with AV input to analyse both processing and acquisition (i.e. vocabulary learning) was conducted by Bisson et al. (2014). English native speakers exposed to a 25-minute video in three subtitling conditions (standard reversed and intralingual subtitles, with Dutch as L2) showed a regular pattern of reading independently of experimental condition. The eye-tracking data revealed that the fixation time in subtitles area was significantly longer with Dutch audio than with English audio (for both English and Dutch subtitles) (p < .001), but no significant differences were found for fixation durations between reversed and intralingual subtitles. Thus, participants read the subtitles equally in English and in Dutch (when the audio was in an unfamiliar language). In addition, when compared to a control group that watched the excerpt without subtitles, it was shown that the experimental groups spent significantly more time in the subtitling area (p < .001). Interestingly, although the average fixation durations were slightly higher in intralingual and reversed subtitling conditions, these differences did not reach significance (p > .68). Consequently, eye tracker analysis showed a regular pattern in subtitles reading independently on the condition, suggesting that adults read subtitles even in an unknown FL and proving the potential of AV input for processing novel language at FE.  
Finally, Lång (2016) used eye-tracking to compare information processing from three available AV sources, that is, sound, image and subtitles. In the study, one group of Finnish native speakers with no knowledge of Russian and a group of Russian native speakers with no knowledge of Finnish watched a 7-minute documentary in Russian with Finnish subtitles. Afterwards, they completed a questionnaire measuring their comprehension of information available in image only, sound only and subtitles only to compare their processing in all three sources. N significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of questions concerning both information available in the image and in the subtitles. Researchers conclude that these findings indicate that:
Since the same information was available in both channels but the participants in the different groups could only effectively follow one of the channels, the results suggest that both types of verbal channels – the visual-verbal and audio-verbal channel – are equally effective as channels for acquiring information. (Lång, 2016, p. 75)
On the other hand, eye-tracking data revealed that Russian speakers spent significantly less time in the subtitles area than Finnish speakers, however, their fixation durations in the subtitles area were significantly longer than in the case of Finnish speakers. Although they were not familiar with the language of subtitles and they were provided with auditory information in their L1, they still spent around 40% of the viewing time in the subtitles area. This demonstrates the capacity of on-screen text in an unfamiliar language to capture viewers’ attention, notwithstanding its limited contribution to comprehension. 
In sum, D’Ydewalle and De Bruycker (2007), Bisson et al. (2014) and Lång (2016) proved that on-screen text is read and processed in any subtitling condition, even when the FL in the subtitles is completely unknown for the viewers. Nevertheless, eye-tracking research so far focused on whether on screen text is read at FE to AV input, without attempting to connect processing to acquisition. In other words, it has not yet been explored whether on-screen text reading at FE leads to novel vocabulary acquisition. For instance, studies on experienced learners suggested that captions processing facilitates form recognition (Montero Pérez et al., 2015), form recall (Puimège et al. 2021), as well as meaning recognition (Wang & Pellicer Sánchez, 2022). Meaning recall, however, was only found to be connected to attention distribution in the case bilingual subtitles (Wang & Pellicer Sánchez, 2022), suggesting that deep processing of meaning requires an additional support of the L1. 
Therefore, previous research with en route learners consistently shows evidence of facilitating effect of on-screen text processing on vocabulary acquisition. However, processing may differ in the case of FE learners not accustomed to TL written form: despite the evidence of captions processing at FE, little is known about whether processing at the very initial stages can enhance vocabulary learning. Consequently, investigation into online processing of on-screen text by ab initio learners is fundamental to elucidate real-time processes unfolding during exposure to AV input. While VLS reveal learners’ conscious intent and vocabulary tests measure the outcome of learning from input, eye-tracking technology offers a unique opportunity to see the input through the eyes of the novice learner., thereby quantifying their attentional distribution.
